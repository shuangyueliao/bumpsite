# Just Diagnosed With Gestational Diabetes? Your First 7 Days, Step by Step

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Getting a gestational diabetes diagnosis in the middle of a pregnancy is a lot to absorb in one phone call. The week after it is mostly paperwork, small habits, and one or two appointments — and every one of those steps produces information worth keeping. This walkthrough covers your first 7 days as a set of things to **write down**, not things to fix. Your care team decides your treatment; your job this week is to become the person with the numbers ready.

## What just happened — in plain language

Gestational diabetes (GD) is diabetes that is found for the first time during pregnancy. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) puts it simply: "Some women develop diabetes for the first time during pregnancy. This condition is called gestational diabetes (GD)" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)).

It typically shows up in mid-pregnancy. The CDC notes that gestational diabetes "usually develops around the 24th week of pregnancy," which is why testing usually happens between weeks 24 and 28 ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)).

And it is not about willpower or anything you did wrong. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) explains that pregnancy hormones and body changes make the body's cells use insulin less well — a condition called insulin resistance — and "all pregnant women have some insulin resistance during late pregnancy"; most people can make enough extra insulin to overcome it, and some cannot ([NIDDK — Gestational Diabetes: Symptoms & Causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/symptoms-causes)).

One more fact worth copying down: ACOG states that "GD goes away after childbirth," though women who have had it are at higher risk of diabetes later in life and should have a blood test 4 to 12 weeks after giving birth ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)).

## Days 0–1: capture the instructions you were given

Between the diagnosis call and your first follow-up, the most useful thing you can do is turn what was said into writing:

- **The check schedule.** How many blood sugar checks per day, and at which times (for example: waking up, and after meals — your team's plan is what counts).
- **Your target numbers, if they were given.** Write down each number and which check it belongs to, plus the unit: mg/dL (US labs and meters) or mmol/L (common elsewhere). If your meter and your lab report use different units, write both down. (Our [OGTT and units guide](/articles/glucose-tolerance-test-pregnancy) explains the two systems.)
- **The appointment list.** Follow-up visit date, dietitian referral if one was made, and where to call with questions.
- **A photo of your lab result.** Your result sheet is the anchor document for everything that follows.

If target numbers were not given yet, don't borrow them from the internet — they're a medical instruction, and yours will come from your care team. (We explain why on our [targets page](/articles/gestational-diabetes-blood-sugar-targets).)

## Days 2–3: set up your logging routine

Home blood sugar checking is a standard part of GDM care: ACOG says you'll use a glucose meter to test your levels, and the CDC lists "checking your blood sugar to make sure your levels stay in a healthy range" among the ways GDM is managed ([ACOG FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes); [CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)).

A log entry only helps later if it has three things: the **reading**, the **check type** (fasting, 1 hour after a meal, 2 hours after a meal, bedtime — whichever your team assigned), and the **time**. Post-meal checks are timed from when you start eating, so also note the time you took the first bite of each meal — that's the anchor every after-meal number hangs on.

Add a short context note when something was different: a long walk after dinner, a rough night of sleep, a delayed lunch. These notes are not judgments. They are the context that makes a week of numbers readable — by you and by your care team.

## Days 4–7: turn your questions into a list

By the end of the first week, most people have a handful of half-formed questions. Write them down as they occur to you and bring the list to your first follow-up — a written list turns a nervous 10-minute visit into an organized one.

## The minimum log, field by field

- **Reading + check type + date and time** — the core record.
- **Meals with first-bite times** — keep food notes rough; a dietitian is the one who reads them.
- **Context notes** — activity, sleep, anything unusual.
- **Medication names and times, if your team prescribes any** — record only. The log never suggests starting, stopping, or changing anything; that decision lives with your care team.

## Questions to bring to your next appointment

1. "What target numbers should I use for fasting and after meals — and in which units, mg/dL or mmol/L?"
2. "How many checks per day, and at exactly which times?"
3. "Who do I contact between appointments if a reading or symptom concerns me?"
4. "Can you refer me to a registered dietitian to build my eating plan?" (The CDC describes a healthy eating plan as something developed *with* your doctor or dietitian — [CDC — Diabetes During Pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-diabetes/index.html).)
