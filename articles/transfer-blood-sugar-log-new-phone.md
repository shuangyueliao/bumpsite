# New Phone, Same Log: Backing Up and Moving Your GDM Reading History

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

If you're mid-pregnancy and gestational diabetes has added blood sugar checks to your daily rhythm, your phone has quietly become one of the most important objects you own: it holds the readings you'll show at your next prenatal visit. So if a new phone is coming — an upgrade, a cracked screen, a hand-me-down — pause before the switch and think about that log.

[ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) tells patients to "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit," and notes that "Blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn." The log isn't a private diary; it's part of the record your care team works from. The reassuring part: transferring a blood sugar log to a new phone is usually a ten-minute job — done *before* you let go of the old phone.

## Why your reading history is worth carrying over

One reading is a moment; the log is a trend. Official guidance treats tracking as routine care: the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html) lists "Checking your blood sugar to make sure your levels stay in a healthy range" among what treatment can involve, and the [ADA](https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/gestational-diabetes) describes management that may include "daily blood glucose testing."

Your care team reads the shape of your weeks, which is why an accidental gap matters less than it may feel: a log that survives the switch, gaps and all, is already doing its job.

## Know where your log actually lives

The right way to move your history depends on where it lives. Most setups fall into one of three homes:

1. **Inside the logging app itself, on your device.** Entries stay in the app's own storage, with no account behind them.
2. **In an account with cloud sync.** You sign in; entries are copied to the developer's servers so they can follow you between devices.
3. **In a connected system.** A phone's built-in health store, or a paired glucose meter's memory, may hold copies too.

One question usually tells you which you have: does the app ask you to sign in? If yes, there's probably a cloud copy; if no, the phone is your history's only home — which makes the export below more important. For how to tell them apart, and what each means for privacy, see our guide to [gestational diabetes app privacy](/articles/gestational-diabetes-app-privacy/).

## The pre-switch checklist (about ten minutes)

Run this while the old phone still works:

1. **Check whether your logging app has an account or sync.** If so, sign in elsewhere and confirm a recent reading shows up.
2. **Export a CSV if the app offers one — even if you also sync.** A CSV opens in any spreadsheet and outlives any app.
3. **Check your meter's memory, if you use one.** Many glucose meters store past readings on the device itself, independent of any phone.
4. **Scroll back to your earliest entries.** Spot-check that week-one readings are still there before you trust the transfer.
5. **Save the export somewhere the new phone can reach.** Email it to yourself or drop it in a cloud folder.
6. **Keep the old phone charged — and un-erased.** It's your fallback until the new device has been verified.

## Moving the log to the new phone

- **If your app has an account:** install it on the new phone, sign in with the same credentials, and give it time to sync. Then verify (below).
- **If your app is on-device only:** use your phone's standard device-to-device transfer during setup — on most phones it generally carries app data along — then open the app and check. Either way, your exported CSV is the bridge.
- **If you're switching platforms** (iPhone to Android or the reverse): built-in health stores generally don't cross between platforms, so the app's own account or export is the dependable route — this is where the CSV earns its keep.
- **Verify with three known readings.** Pick your earliest entry, one from the middle, and one from this week; confirm the dates and values on the new phone.

Only after that verification, erase the old phone with your operating system's full erase process.

## If something didn't make it across

First, don't panic: a partial log is still a useful log. Then, in order: re-export and import again; check your meter's memory; contact the app's support team — migration is a common support question; and re-enter missing stretches from paper logs or screenshots. If there's a gap, bring the CSV or a printed report to your next visit — your care team works with patterns, not perfection, and a small hole in a well-kept record is normal and fixable.

## FAQ

**Is it safe to sync my pregnancy blood sugar data to an account?**
There's no single right answer. The trade: cloud sync makes moving phones easier; on-device storage keeps your log in your hands. Our [privacy guide](/articles/gestational-diabetes-app-privacy/) walks through the questions worth asking before you commit weeks of data to an app.

**Will my log move between an iPhone and an Android phone?**
Usually not automatically: the two systems' health platforms are separate, and on-device apps can't carry data across by themselves. A CSV from the old setup is the most portable option; whether the new side can import it depends on the app.

**What happens to my log after the baby arrives?**
The record keeps value after delivery: the [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational) notes, "If you had gestational diabetes, you are more likely to develop type 2 diabetes," which is one reason follow-up continues after birth.
