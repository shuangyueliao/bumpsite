# Insulin Notes in Your GDM Log: What to Record — and What You Should Never Decide Yourself

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

If your care team adds insulin to your gestational diabetes plan, your log takes on a second job: alongside the glucose readings, it becomes the record of what was taken, when, and how it fit the day. This page is about that *record* — and it draws one line early and never crosses it: what to take, how much, and when are your care team's decisions alone. You will find no doses here, no regimen comparisons, and no adjustment advice, because that's not a writer's job, an app's job, or the internet's job.

## Why insulin enters the picture — in the officials' words

The sequence is standard enough that the authorities describe it in patient-facing language. NIDDK: "Managing gestational diabetes includes following a healthy eating plan and being physically active. If your eating plan and physical activity aren't enough to keep your blood glucose in your target range, you may need insulin" ([NIDDK, Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational)). ACOG: "For some women, medications may be needed to manage GD. Insulin is the recommended medication during pregnancy to help women control their blood sugar" ([ACOG, Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)).

Both statements describe *their* decision tree — your team's. Nothing in this article, or in any log-keeping app, belongs inside that decision.

## What the log is for, once insulin is part of the plan

ACOG connects the record to the care directly: "Your ob-gyn should review your glucose log to make sure that the medication is working. Changes to your medication may be needed throughout your pregnancy to help keep your blood sugar in the normal range" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). Read that carefully: the log your team reviews is one of the inputs they use to manage the plan. A clear, complete record makes their job easier; a gap-riddled one forces them to work from memory and impressions.

That's the entire purpose of an insulin log: to be the trustworthy record your care team reviews.

## What to record in an insulin note

Keep it boring, exact, and verbatim-from-your-instructions:

- **The medication name, exactly as your team wrote it.** Copy it from the prescription or after-visit summary, not from memory.
- **The amount, exactly as prescribed — recorded, never chosen.** The dose is your team's instruction; your log's job is to capture what was instructed and what actually happened.
- **The time it was taken.** Actual clock time, not "morning."
- **Deviations, recorded without self-judgment:** taken late, taken early, a dose that couldn't be taken as instructed (and when you told the team, or that you need to).
- **The glucose readings around it**, tagged with their check types, so the record your team reviews shows the full picture in one place.

And one meta-rule: if anything about the instructions is ambiguous — two numbers on the label, a timing you're unsure of — that's a question for your care team *before* the next dose, not a guess in a log.

## What you should never decide yourself

This list is shorter and more important:

- **Never start, stop, or change any medication or dose** based on a log reading, an app, an article, or a community thread. The [NIDDK page](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment) that describes when insulin may be needed is addressed to care teams' decision-making, not to self-management experiments.
- **Never compare your regimen** with a friend's, a forum post's, or another app's "typical" plan and adjust toward it. Pregnancies differ; regimens are individual instructions.
- **Never skip or delay a dose** to "see what happens" with the numbers. If a dose was missed, record it and contact your team per the instructions they gave you — which is itself worth asking about in advance (see the questions below).

The pattern in all three: the log is a *record and communication* tool. Judgment lives with the clinicians.

## The record your team reviews

ACOG's line — "Your ob-gyn should review your glucose log" — means the end consumer of your log is a clinician with limited time. The same standards apply as for any GDM log: every entry timed and typed, notes attached to context, one consistent unit ([the full report format is here](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report/)). Medication notes belong *inside* that same record, next to the readings they relate to — not in a separate notebook that has to be cross-referenced at every visit.

## Questions to ask when insulin enters the plan

- "Can you write down the exact medication name, amounts, and times, so I can record them verbatim in my log?"
- "If I take a dose late, or miss one, what should I do — and what should I record?"
- "How do you want the log organized at visits — readings and medication notes together, or separate?"
- "What side effects or reactions would you want me to contact you about right away?"

That last question is deliberately open-ended: what counts as urgent is precisely the kind of judgment your care team should hand you personally, in their words — never an app's, and never an article's.

Keep the record boring, bring it everywhere, and let the [readable report](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report/) do the talking at visits. BumpSugar's [waitlist](/#waitlist) is open if you want a log that keeps the boundary as strictly as this page does.
