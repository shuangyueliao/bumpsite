# How Often to Check Blood Sugar With Gestational Diabetes: Your Team Sets the Number — a System Makes It Happen

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

One of the first questions after a gestational diabetes diagnosis is a number: how many times a day will I need to check my blood sugar? The direct answer: the count is not printed on any official page, and it is not yours to pick — it is written into the plan your care team gives you. This article covers both halves: who sets the frequency, and how reminders plus honest logging make the prescribed checks happen.

## The short answer: the count is your care team's to set

Checking at home is standard care. The CDC lists "Checking your blood sugar to make sure your levels stay in a healthy range" among the ways gestational diabetes is managed during pregnancy ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html), accessed 2026-10-30), and the NIH's NIDDK describes the tool: "Your health care team may ask you to use a blood glucose meter to check your blood glucose levels" ([NIDDK — Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed 2026-10-30).

Notice the phrasing: *may ask*. The meter is standard; the schedule around it is not. The ADA frames targets the same way: something "you and your healthcare team will decide," with example numbers offered only "for most women with GDM" ([ADA — How to Treat Gestational Diabetes](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/pregnancy/gestational-diabetes/how-to-treat-gestational-diabetes), accessed 2026-10-30). ACOG's patient page offers no count, just a record-keeping instruction: "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed 2026-10-30). We checked all four official pages while writing this: none publishes a one-size-fits-all daily count. The schedule is a prescription — and prescriptions come from your clinicians, ideally in writing.

## Why the number is individual

Two plans for two people with the same due date can look very different, by design:

- **Your plan tracks your numbers.** Your team adjusts the schedule around your own readings — which is why they want the log at each visit.
- **Check types differ.** Some plans center on fasting checks, some add after-meal checks, some a bedtime slot. If yours uses after-meal checks, the next question is when the clock starts — covered in [our after-meal timing guide](/articles/when-to-check-blood-sugar-after-eating-pregnancy).

A frequency copied from a forum, a friend, or another app's default is not your plan — and neither are borrowed target numbers ([why we won't print targets as advice](/articles/gestational-diabetes-blood-sugar-targets)). If yours isn't written down anywhere, that is not a you-problem: it is the first thing to request at your next appointment.

## Write the schedule down like the prescription it is

Turn your team's number into five lines you can find at 6 a.m.:

- **How many checks per day** — the count, in their words.
- **When each one happens** — by clock or by events (waking, meals, bedtime).
- **The units** — mg/dL (US meters) or mmol/L (common elsewhere).
- **What counts as "done"** — how late checks should be marked.
- **Who to ask** — when anything about the plan is unclear.

## A reminder system that matches the plan — instead of fighting it

App reviews keep surfacing one failure: what breaks a prescribed schedule is rarely the finger pricks — it is reminders that don't match anyone's real plan. Alerts at times your team didn't mention, pings you can't rename or move, so many of them that swiping becomes a reflex — until the one that mattered gets swiped too.

A setup that holds up for months has a few properties — none of them clinical:

- **Mirrors your schedule, slot by slot** — each reminder labeled ("after-lunch check"), at your team's offset.
- **Editable in seconds** — when your team moves a slot, the reminder moves too.
- **One tap to a record** — a reminder that flows into the log closes the loop; one that only nags teaches you to ignore it.
- **Late-friendly** — a slipped check should be a five-second save, not a lost reading.
- **One layer, not the whole plan** — iOS notifications can be delayed or suppressed by Focus modes, silent mode, or permissions; any single alert can be missed. Anchor reminders to routines that exist anyway: your own alarm, the meal itself.

## When a check slips (it happens to everyone)

Miss one, and the move is small: log it anyway, with the actual time and a note that it ran late. NIDDK's instruction is simply "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose" — an honest log with a visible gap is more useful than a tidy-looking one with quiet holes — your team can only read what you recorded. Unsure whether a late check still counts? That is a two-line question for your team, not a midnight judgment call.

## Frequently asked questions

**So how many times a day should I check?**
However many times your care team wrote into your plan — that is the only answer we can stand behind. The CDC, NIDDK, ADA, and ACOG pages describe the meter and the record-keeping, not a universal count.

**What should I do if I miss a check?**
Log it with its actual time and a note that it was late, then carry on with the day. If you are unsure whether to test late or wait for the next slot, ask your team.

**Will my checking schedule change over the pregnancy?**
It can, as your team adjusts the plan during pregnancy — ACOG's phrasing is that you continue monitoring "as recommended," with your ob-gyn reviewing your glucose log.

**Can an app handle the reminders for me?**
A good app carries a lot — reminders matched to your slots, one-tap logging, easy late entries — but no notification system is delivery-proof on a phone with Focus modes, silent switches, and permission toggles. Treat reminders as one layer over your own routine.
