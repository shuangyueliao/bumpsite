# Gestational Diabetes in the Second Trimester: From the Screening Window to Your First Month of Logging

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Between weeks 24 and 28, gestational diabetes usually moves from something you've heard about to something on your calendar: the routine glucose screen lands in this window, and for some readers, so does a diagnosis. This article walks that arc — what the window is, what the screen does and doesn't decide, and how to build your first month of records, including the part nobody warns you about: logging meals while your second-trimester appetite has plans of its own.

## Why gestational diabetes tends to show up now

The CDC puts it plainly: gestational diabetes "usually develops around the 24th week of pregnancy, so you'll likely be tested between 24 and 28 weeks" ([CDC — Diabetes During Pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-diabetes/index.html)). The cause is hormonal, not personal: the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) explains that "all pregnant women have some insulin resistance during late pregnancy" ([NIDDK — Gestational Diabetes: Symptoms & Causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/symptoms-causes)). Nothing you ate or didn't do caused this. And because "usually, gestational diabetes has no symptoms," per NIDDK, the screen — not symptoms — is how most people find out.

## The screening step: one checkpoint, not a verdict

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) describes the default plan: "your blood sugar will be measured between 24 and 28 weeks of pregnancy" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)).

Two topics live on their own pages: test day itself in our [glucose tolerance test guide](/articles/glucose-tolerance-test-pregnancy); and what a high result means — a next step, not a verdict — in our [failed the glucose test guide](/articles/failed-glucose-test-pregnancy). A result above your lab's cutoff starts a **process**; the interpretation belongs to your care team. Don't change how you eat to "prepare" for the test without asking your provider first.

## If the diagnosis comes: week one is a recording week

A diagnosis adds one daily job: recording. The CDC lists "checking your blood sugar to make sure your levels stay in a healthy range" among the ways gestational diabetes is managed ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)) — so your team will hand you a checking plan of their own design. Your part is capture, not interpretation:

- **The instructions, in writing** — how many checks per day, at which times, as your team said them.
- **Target numbers, only if your team gave them.** If none were given yet, don't borrow numbers from the internet (see [why targets belong to your care team](/articles/gestational-diabetes-blood-sugar-targets)).
- **Every reading with its check type and time** — NIDDK's instruction is simply to "record the results every time you check your blood glucose" ([NIDDK — Gestational Diabetes: Management & Treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)).

For a day-by-day version, see our [first 7 days after diagnosis](/articles/just-diagnosed-gestational-diabetes).

## A first-month rhythm: three checkpoints

Month one of logging is a data collection month, not a willpower test:

**Week 1 — scaffolding.** Every reading gets three fields: the number, the check type your team assigned, and the time. Every meal gets a first-bite time.

**Weeks 2–3 — consistency over precision.** Log right after eating; rough descriptions are fine ("rice, beans, chicken" is enough). Add a context note when something was different — a walk, a bad night, a late lunch. The American Diabetes Association suggests you "write down your results and note what factors may have affected them, such as food, activity, and stress" ([ADA — Checking Your Blood Sugar](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/treatment-care/checking-your-blood-sugar)).

**Week 4 — look back before the visit.** ACOG's reason for all of it: "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). A month of meals and readings in time order turns the visit into a specific conversation.

## Logging meals when your appetite has opinions

Nobody briefs you on this part: by mid-pregnancy, many people notice appetite arriving in waves — hungrier some hours, meals shrinking, snacks multiplying. The fix isn't discipline; it's a log shaped like your actual eating.

The official pages quietly agree. ACOG notes that "often, three meals and two to three snacks per day are recommended" in GDM plans ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)) — so your log needs snack rows, not just meal rows. The CDC describes eating as "healthy food in the right amounts at the right times" ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)) — and *at the right times* is why the first-bite time is the valuable half of every entry. The plan isn't yours to design: "Your health care team will help you make a healthy eating plan with food choices that are good for you and your baby" ([NIDDK — Management & Treatment](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)). Your job is the record: log within minutes of eating, stamp the first-bite time, note snacks too, and attach each after-meal reading to its meal. For the full method, see our [GDM food log guide](/articles/gestational-diabetes-food-log).

## Walks and context

Movement is part of the picture: "Being active lowers your blood sugar and makes you more sensitive to insulin," per the CDC ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)). ACOG likes one version in particular: "it's a good idea to add a walk for 10 to 15 minutes after each meal" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)) — what applies to you, especially later in pregnancy, is your team's call. Log activity as a **context note**: "walked after dinner" beside a reading is exactly the pairing your team can use.

## FAQ

**My glucose screen is scheduled this week — should I start logging already?**
No readings to log yet — nothing is required today. If noting meals steadies your nerves, it's harmless data; just don't change how you eat to influence the result without asking your provider. What a high result means is in our [failed the glucose test guide](/articles/failed-glucose-test-pregnancy).

**I was diagnosed but haven't been given target numbers. What do I log?**
Everything except targets: the check schedule, every reading with its check type and time, meal times, questions as they occur to you. Targets are medical instructions from your care team — see [why we don't print them here](/articles/gestational-diabetes-blood-sugar-targets).

**Do I have to weigh portions or count carbs for the food log?**
Not unless your dietitian asks. A rough description with a first-bite time is a usable record — rough-and-consistent beats precise-then-abandoned. The method is in our [food log guide](/articles/gestational-diabetes-food-log).

**When does all this logging end?**
ACOG notes that "GD goes away after childbirth, but women who have had GD are at higher risk of developing diabetes later in life" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). Your team will plan testing after birth — an archived, exportable log is useful then (see [after birth: archiving your GDM log](/articles/after-birth-gdm-log-archive)).
