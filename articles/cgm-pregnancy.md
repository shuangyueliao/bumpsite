# Using a CGM During Pregnancy: What Flows Into Your Log (and What Still Needs a Fingerstick)

Continuous glucose monitors — CGMs — have become a familiar piece of diabetes care, and your care team may raise the idea during a gestational diabetes pregnancy. This article doesn't review devices or compare brands — whether a CGM is right for you is a conversation with your clinician. It answers the question that follows the decision: **how does CGM data coexist with the fingerstick log you've been building?**

## What a CGM does, in the official words

The NIH's NIDDK describes the device plainly: "A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) estimates what your glucose level is every few minutes and keeps track of it over time," and "CGMs are always on and recording glucose levels — whether you're showering, working, exercising, or sleeping" ([niddk.nih.gov — Continuous Glucose Monitoring](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/managing-diabetes/continuous-glucose-monitoring), accessed October 29, 2026).

Three parts make that happen, per the same page:

- A **tiny sensor** inserted under the skin — often the belly or arm — held by a sticky patch.
- A **transmitter** that sends readings wirelessly.
- A **software program on a smartphone** (or pump or separate receiver) that displays the stream.

One word in that description deserves attention: *estimates*. The same page explains why — "CGM sensors estimate the glucose level in the fluid between your cells, which is very similar to the glucose level in your blood." Similar, not identical. Hold that thought; it's the reason fingersticks don't disappear.

Who gets one, and for how long, is a medical decision made with your team. The NIDDK notes that "doctors can prescribe CGMs," that "your doctor may suggest using a CGM all the time or only for a few days to help adjust your diabetes care," and — the sentence worth remembering — "Talk with your doctor about whether using a CGM could help you manage your diabetes" ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/managing-diabetes/continuous-glucose-monitoring), accessed October 29, 2026).

## What still needs a fingerstick

Even with a sensor attached, meter checks remain part of the picture in official guidance:

- The NIDDK's CGM page says that for some models "you may need to do a finger-stick test with a standard blood glucose monitor to calibrate the system," and that "you may sometimes need to compare your CGM glucose readings with a finger-stick test" ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/managing-diabetes/continuous-glucose-monitoring), accessed October 29, 2026).
- ACOG's patient page on gestational diabetes describes meter testing as the core checking tool: "You will use a glucose meter to test your blood sugar levels. This device measures blood sugar from a small drop of blood" ([acog.org — Gestational Diabetes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed October 29, 2026).

The practical reading: in a GDM pregnancy, the prescribed schedule of meter checks — fasting, after-meal, bedtime, however your team wrote it — stays the backbone of your log. Whether a CGM joins it, for how long, and how your team wants the two streams weighted are questions for your appointments. What this article can do is keep your *records* straight when both exist.

## The data-flow view: two streams, one log

Think of your log as having two tributaries:

**Stream 1 — scheduled meter checks.** You run these per your prescription: exact time, check type (fasting, 1-hour, 2-hour, bedtime), the value, the meal it followed, a line of context. These are the entries your team's schedule names explicitly — the same entries covered in our [after-meal timing guide](/articles/when-to-check-blood-sugar-after-eating-pregnancy/).

**Stream 2 — CGM readings.** Many CGM systems send their readings into Apple Health. If yours does, BumpSugar can import them automatically — read-only — so the stream lands in your log without retyping (this import is part of the subscription; everything works during the 14-day trial). Two rules are built in: imported readings are **tagged by source**, and BumpSugar **never writes anything back** to Apple Health.

Why the source tag matters: a meter check is a one-moment measure of blood; a CGM reading is an estimate of the fluid between your cells, refreshed every few minutes. Both are true statements about different samples — the NIDDK quotes above say exactly this — and your care team will read them differently. A log that keeps the streams labeled lets them do that; a log that blends them into one undifferentiated column takes that ability away.

## Recording rules when both exist

- **Don't overwrite one source with the other.** A fingerstick and a CGM value taken at the same moment are two readings from two methods. Keep both, each labeled — they can legitimately differ.
- **Attach check types to meter checks.** The schedule your team prescribed ("fasting," "1 hour after eating") refers to meter checks. Enter those with their check types; let the CGM stream be the background context unless your team says otherwise.
- **Mistakes stay fixable.** A mislabeled or late entry — from either stream — should be editable after the fact, in full: time, type, meal link. (Why that matters and what refusal looks like in practice: [reading your log](/articles/blood-sugar-log-pregnancy/).)
- **Ask how the report should look.** Some clinicians want scheduled checks only; others welcome the full CGM stream. One question at your next visit settles it.
