# A High Reading in Your GDM Log: What to Write Down Before You Worry

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

The 6am fasting number comes back higher than the target your care team gave you, and the spiral starts before the coffee. This page won't tell you what the number means — that conversation belongs to your care team. It will tell you what to do in the next ten minutes so that whatever the number means, your log is ready to explain it.

## First: one reading is one reading

Care teams work from records, not single data points. NIDDK's guidance is built around the log, not the lone result: "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose. Your blood glucose records can help you and your health care team decide whether your diabetes care plan is working" ([NIDDK, Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)). A single high fasting value is an entry. A week of them is a pattern. The whole job, right now, is making sure nothing about that entry gets lost before it can become part of the pattern.

## Step 1 — write the context, not the verdict

The reading alone is the least interesting thing on the page. What makes it useful is everything around it:

- **Exact time of the reading** — a 6:10am fasting check and a 8:45am "fasting" check are different data.
- **Last night's timing.** When did you eat dinner? Was it later, larger, or different from usual?
- **Sleep.** Rough night, short night — worth three words.
- **The morning so far.** Anything unusual before the test: a 5am wake-up, medication timing per your team's instructions (record what happened — decisions stay with your team).
- **Which check type it was.** Fasting readings and after-meal readings tell different stories and should never share a column.

None of this is interpretation. It's the raw material your OB or endocrinologist will want at the next visit — [here's the report format they can actually use](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report/).

## Step 2 — separate the streaks by check type

The second high fasting number in a row means something different from a high after dinner followed by a normal fasting. That's why your log should keep check types apart:

- **Fasting streak** — several mornings in a row over your target. This is the pattern most worth bringing to your team as its own topic, because it's the one least explained by last night's dinner.
- **After-meal streak** — several readings over target after similar meals. The food notes next to those readings are the useful part.
- **One-off readings** — a single high number with a clear story (birthday dinner, terrible sleep, late meal) often just needs its note.

What the patterns *mean* medically is not knowable from a log and not this article's business. What the patterns are — that's the part a log is built to show, and it's the part you control.

## Step 3 — turn the pattern into a specific question

"My numbers have been high" gives your care team nothing to work with at the visit. This gives them everything:

> "My fasting readings have been over my target 4 of the last 7 mornings. The notes show two late dinners and one bad-sleep night among them. What should we look at?"

Notice what that question does: it states your recorded facts, against the targets *your team set*, and asks for their read. It doesn't diagnose, doesn't borrow a threshold from the internet, and doesn't preempt their judgment. If you keep an [appointment agenda](/articles/gestational-diabetes-questions-to-ask-doctor/), this question goes at the top.

## What not to do with a high reading

- **Don't change your food, activity, or medication routine because of one number.** NIDDK is explicit about where those decisions live: "Your blood glucose records can help you and your health care team decide whether your diabetes care plan is working" ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)) — *help them decide*, not help you pre-decide. Any change to medication in particular starts and ends with your care team.
- **Don't quietly add extra checks** without asking your team — the check schedule is part of their instruction, and "what should I do if I miss one / want to change one" is a perfect [agenda question](/articles/gestational-diabetes-questions-to-ask-doctor/).
- **Don't erase the reading or round it down.** The typo-fix and the wishful-thinking-fix are different edits. Fix errors; record reality.
- **Don't grade yourself.** A log is an instrument panel, not a report card. The instrument panel's job is to be honest.

## Questions to bring to your care team

- "My fasting readings were over my target on X of the last 7 mornings — here's the log with notes. What should we look at?"
- "Is there anything beyond food, timing, and sleep you want me to note next to a high reading?"
- "If a pattern shows up between visits, how do you want me to reach you — and how quickly?"
