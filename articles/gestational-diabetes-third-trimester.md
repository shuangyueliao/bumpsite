# Gestational Diabetes in the Third Trimester: Weekly Visits, Insulin Conversations, and What to Bring

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

The third trimester changes the rhythm of gestational diabetes care: appointments arrive in quick succession, blood sugar checks become a weekly routine, and for many people insulin joins the conversation. Treatment decisions belong to your OB and your care team — your part is preparation. This article covers the visit rhythm, what to record between visits, and what to bring to each appointment.

## Why the visits get closer together

The tightening schedule is by design. MedlinePlus, the National Library of Medicine's patient-information service, describes the usual pattern: "In your third trimester, you will have a prenatal visit every 2 weeks until week 36," and "After that, you will see your doctor or midwife every week" ([MedlinePlus — Prenatal care in your third trimester](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000558.htm)). Gestational diabetes adds its own reason to be seen often: ACOG tells patients, "You will need more frequent prenatal care visits to monitor your health and your fetus's health" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). If your calendar fills up, that is the plan working — not something going wrong. And the CDC's between-visit instruction fits in one line: "see your doctor as recommended and monitor your blood sugar levels" ([CDC — Diabetes During Pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-diabetes/index.html)).

## What happens at the visit — and what is yours to do

At a typical third-trimester check, your provider runs their own checklist: MedlinePlus lists weighing you, checking your blood pressure, and measuring your abdomen "to see if your baby is growing as expected" ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000558.htm)). Your checklist is shorter and just as important: bring the record. ACOG is direct — "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)).

These visits are short, so format matters: your OB will scan for patterns rather than scroll weeks of phone entries — a one- or two-page report organized by check type can be read in the minutes available. We show exactly how to build one in [How to Turn Your GDM Log Book Into a 2-Page Report Your Doctor Will Actually Read](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report).

## What to record in the weeks between visits

Home checking stays the backbone — the CDC lists "Checking your blood sugar to make sure your levels stay in a healthy range" among the ways gestational diabetes is managed ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)). And the record is a shared tool, not busywork: NIDDK notes that records "can help you and your health care team decide whether your diabetes care plan is working," and to "Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([NIDDK — Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)).

A useful record has four small habits:

- **Reading + check type + date and time** — fasting, after a meal, or bedtime, whichever your team assigned; if after-meal checks are timed from the start of eating, note when each meal started.
- **Meals as rough notes** — a dietitian reads these, not a judge.
- **Context** — a walk after dinner, a rough night of sleep, a delayed lunch — context turns a number into a readable day.
- **Medications exactly as prescribed, if any** — names, times, amounts *as written by your team*: recorded, never decided by you or an app.

Plus one running item: questions, written down as they occur.

## If insulin comes up: prepare for the conversation, not a verdict

For many people, the third trimester is when medication joins the discussion — the official guidance describes a planned step, not a crisis. NIDDK: "If your eating plan and physical activity aren't enough to keep your blood glucose in your target range, you may need insulin" ([NIDDK — Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational)). ACOG: "Insulin is the recommended medication during pregnancy to help women control their blood sugar" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)).

For peace of mind, one piece of physiology: "All pregnant women have some insulin resistance during late pregnancy," per NIDDK ([NIDDK — Symptoms & Causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/symptoms-causes)) — pregnancy hormones do this to everyone; needing medication is not a verdict on your effort.

The line is simple: what to take, how much, and when to change anything are decisions only your care team makes. Yours is the supporting role — record exactly what is prescribed (name, amount as written, timing), and keep the log current, because "Your ob-gyn should review your glucose log to make sure that the medication is working" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). For what belongs in a medication record — and what should never be decided inside a log — see [Insulin Notes in Your GDM Log](/articles/insulin-log).

## The appointment-ready checklist

Before you leave for each visit:

- your log — ideally a short, scannable report ([here's how to build one](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report))
- your meter, if your team asked you to bring it
- any medications as currently prescribed, with names and times
- your questions list — last visit's items first
- anything unusual since last time: symptoms, missed checks, schedule disruptions

## FAQ

### How often will my prenatal visits be in the third trimester with gestational diabetes?

The usual pattern is "a prenatal visit every 2 weeks until week 36," then weekly (MedlinePlus) — and gestational diabetes typically adds monitoring: ACOG says more frequent visits are needed for your health and your baby's. Your team's schedule is the one to follow.

### What should I bring to each appointment?

The most useful item is your blood sugar record — ACOG asks patients to "keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit." Add your meter if asked, your medication list as prescribed, and your questions list.

### My doctor mentioned insulin. Does that mean diet and exercise failed?

It means what NIDDK describes: "If your eating plan and physical activity aren't enough to keep your blood glucose in your target range, you may need insulin" — a standard step in a body state every pregnancy shares. Doses and changes are your care team's decisions; yours is to record what is prescribed and keep the log they review.

### Does my logging change in the third trimester?

The core stays the same — reading, check type, date and time. What grows is everything around it: extra checks, medication entries if prescribed, more visits to prepare for. NIDDK's framing holds at any stage: records "can help you and your health care team decide whether your diabetes care plan is working."
