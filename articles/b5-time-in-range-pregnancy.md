# Time in range during pregnancy: what TIR can and can't tell you about your log

It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. If you've read anything about diabetes online, you've run into the phrase **time in range** — often shortened to **TIR**. If you're managing gestational diabetes with a glucose meter and a log — not a continuous monitor — it's fair to wonder whether the phrase even applies to you.

Short version: the *idea* travels well, but it deserves a careful explanation. Here's what time in range means, how the same arithmetic works on a fingerstick log, and — just as important — what that percentage can't tell you.

## What "time in range" actually means

The [American Diabetes Association](https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/devices-technology/cgm-time-in-range) defines it plainly: "Time in range is the amount of time you spend in the target blood glucose (blood sugar) range — between 70 and 180 mg/dL for most people." The ADA adds that "most people should aim for a time in range of at least 70 percent of readings," and — importantly — that "some may have different targets" and "there's no universal time in range goal."

Two things deserve emphasis for anyone who is pregnant:

- **The 70–180 mg/dL and "70 percent" figures are general adult framing, not a pregnancy prescription.** The ADA page we're citing says nothing specific about pregnancy or gestational diabetes. During pregnancy, care teams typically use tighter, more individualized targets — the [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment) lists example targets for gestational diabetes (such as "1 hour after eating: 140 or less") and still ends with "Ask your doctor what targets are right for you."
- **"In range" is only ever meaningful relative to a target.** In this article, "your targets" always means the numbers your care team gave you — the ones you enter into your log or app. A percentage computed against anything else is decoration.

## How time in range works on a fingerstick log

TIR grew up alongside continuous glucose monitors, and the [ADA's explainer](https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/devices-technology/cgm-time-in-range) frames it that way. But the underlying arithmetic works on any series of readings — including a log of fingerstick checks done at planned times.

A log-based version looks like this:

1. **The denominator is your planned checks.** If your care team asked for fasting and one post-meal check each day, a week holds 14 planned checks. The denominator is 14 — not "however many you happened to do."
2. **The numerator is the checks that landed within your targets.** Say 12 of the 14 were within the targets you set. That week's time in range is about 86%.
3. **Each check type is counted against its own target.** Fasting and after-meal readings usually have different targets, so a good tool keeps them separate or shows you each slice — blending them into one pot hides the detail your team will ask about.

That's the whole concept: a share of planned checks inside your own targets, over a defined window like a week or two.

## What time in range can tell you

Used carefully, the percentage is genuinely useful:

- **It compresses a week into one glance.** The [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment) says to "record the results every time you check your blood glucose" — a percentage is one way of seeing that whole record at once.
- **It shows direction, not just dots.** Week over week, a rising or falling share of in-range checks tells you whether things are trending the way your care team hopes — a conversation starter, not a verdict.
- **It gives appointments a shared starting point.** Instead of scrolling through entries, you and your care team can start from "last week was about 80% in range against my targets."

## What it can't tell you — and where people misuse it

- **It is not a grade.** A percentage never explains itself. It can't tell you *why* a check landed where it did.
- **Missed checks distort it.** If you skipped the fasting check three days running, your "in range" share quietly improves or worsens for reasons that have nothing to do with your body. That's why the denominator — planned checks — matters more than the percentage itself.
- **Single readings happen.** The [ADA's monitoring guidance](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/treatment-care/checking-your-blood-sugar) tells you to "write down your results and note what factors may have affected them, such as food, activity, and stress." Those factors are exactly what a bare percentage throws away.
- **There is no published pregnancy-specific TIR target on any page we cite here.** Be skeptical of any article that hands you a universal "pregnant TIR goal." Interpreting your percentage belongs to your care team.
- **It doesn't replace your care team's view.** Tools summarize; clinicians interpret.

## How to record so the number means something

- Log **every planned check** — the metric is only as honest as its denominator ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)).
- Tag the **check type** every time (fasting, 1-hour, 2-hour, bedtime), so each reading is compared to the right target.
- For post-meal checks, note the **time you started eating**, so the timing label matches your care team's instruction.
- Add a short **context note** — food, a walk, a rough night — the way the [ADA](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/treatment-care/checking-your-blood-sugar) suggests.
- Review **weekly**, not at every reading.
