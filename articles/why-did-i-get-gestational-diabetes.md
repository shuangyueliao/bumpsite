If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. Two in the morning, third-trimester insomnia, and the search bar: *why did I get gestational diabetes*. Underneath the search for an explanation, you're probably asking whether you did something to cause this. This page won't hand you a list of causes to measure yourself against; the internet has plenty, and they mostly turn into blame checklists. It does something more useful: what can honestly be said about the "why," and how to turn that replaying question into questions your care team can actually answer.

## The honest version of the "why"

The experts start with uncertainty. The American Diabetes Association says it plainly: "The exact cause of GDM is unclear and there's a lot we don't know" ([ADA — Gestational Diabetes](https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/gestational-diabetes)). ACOG's patient page stays definitional: "Some women develop diabetes for the first time during pregnancy" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)).

It is common: the CDC reports that "every year, 5% to 9% of U.S. pregnancies are affected by gestational diabetes" ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)).

That's already steadier than an explanation. If the exact cause isn't fully understood even by the people who study it, the search for "the thing I did wrong" has nothing solid to land on. "Why me" is a question about your specific body and pregnancy — which is why the answer belongs with your care team, not a search result. A diagnosis is information for your care, not a grade on your effort.

## Why this page won't list causes for you

Plenty of pages will give you a checklist of things "linked to" gestational diabetes. This article deliberately leaves the biology out, for a practical reason: when you're hurting, every item on a cause list converts into evidence for the prosecution — until the list reads as a report card. You weren't being graded.

The real explanation lives in two places. One is the official pages from the CDC, NIH's NIDDK, ACOG, and the ADA at the end of this article; the NIDDK's [Symptoms & Causes page](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/symptoms-causes) covers the biology in plain language. The other is your care team: your OB, endocrinologist, or diabetes educator can connect the general explanation to your history and this pregnancy — something no article can do. For a calm starting point first, our [overview of gestational diabetes](/articles/what-is-gestational-diabetes) covers what it is and what your team may ask you to track.

## Three better questions to replace "why me"

You can't argue yourself out of the question, but you can give it a different job:

1. **"Can you help me understand what happened in my body — in terms I could repeat to my partner?"** Understanding is allowed on the agenda; you don't need a perfect question to earn one.
2. **"Given this, what's our plan?"** How your team wants you tracking, what gets reviewed at each visit, who builds your eating plan with you. The forward-facing question is the one with answers attached.
3. **"Who can I talk to if this stops feeling like information and starts feeling like guilt?"** The ADA encourages exactly this: "Don't hesitate to ask questions or seek support—there are many effective ways to manage GDM" ([ADA](https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/gestational-diabetes)). Support is part of standard care.

## The copy-ready list

Compressed for your next appointment:

1. "Can you walk me through what causes gestational diabetes generally, and what, if anything, applies to my case?"
2. "Is there anything about my history or this pregnancy I should understand better now?"
3. "What does managing this look like for me — what will you have me track, and what do we review at each visit?"
4. "Who builds my eating plan with me, and when is that appointment?"
5. "What follow-up should I expect after the baby arrives?" ACOG describes the standard: "If you have GD, you should have a blood test 4 to 12 weeks after you give birth" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)).
6. "Who do I talk to if I'm blaming myself and it's not lifting?"

For the fuller question bank plus a printable appointment agenda, see [Questions to Ask at Your Gestational Diabetes Appointments](/articles/gestational-diabetes-questions-to-ask-doctor) — questions, not answers. Every answer belongs to your care team.

## If the guilt shows up anyway

If "why me" keeps replaying even after a decent appointment, that's normal — it doesn't mean anything has gone wrong. Three things that tend to help:

- **Say it out loud at an appointment.** Guilt grows fastest between visits; naming it once usually shrinks it.
- **Read your log as context, not judgment.** Every reading is a data point your team asked you to collect; the numbers report, they don't accuse.

## FAQ

**Is gestational diabetes my fault?**
The honest, sourced version: it's common — "every year, 5% to 9% of U.S. pregnancies are affected" ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)); in 2021, "about 8% of women who had a live birth developed gestational diabetes" ([CDC — Diabetes During Pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-diabetes/index.html)) — and the ADA describes the exact cause as unclear. No article can grade your choices; the explanation for your case is a conversation with your care team.

**Could I have prevented it?**
That question points backward, and this page won't pretend to answer it — even the official description of the cause includes a lot of "we don't know." The question with answers attached is forward-facing: what the plan looks like now, which only your care team can answer for you.

**Will it go away after the baby is born?**
ACOG says "GD goes away after childbirth," and that people who have had GD should have a blood test 4 to 12 weeks after giving birth ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). Ask your team to schedule it.
