# Telehealth Visits With Gestational Diabetes: How to Show Your Numbers When There's No Paper to Hand Over

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

A video prenatal visit solves some real problems — no waiting room, no commute — and creates a new one: there is no front desk to hand your paper log to. The habit your care team asked for hasn't changed; only the delivery method has. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) tells patients that with gestational diabetes "You will need more frequent prenatal care visits to monitor your health and your fetus's health," and that "Blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn" ([ACOG, Gestational Diabetes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed October 30, 2026). A digital log isn't a workaround for remote visits — it's an officially recognized format for any visit.

MedlinePlus puts the skill in one line: "For telehealth visits, just like with an in-person visit, it is important to be prepared and have good communication with the provider" ([NIH MedlinePlus, Telehealth](https://medlineplus.gov/telehealth.html), accessed October 30, 2026). This article covers the prepared part — before the call, during it, and just after. It deliberately skips how to operate the video software (your clinic will tell you that part); what goes in front of the camera is yours to prepare.

## Before the call: what to send through the patient portal

The biggest upgrade to a telehealth visit happens days before it: get your numbers over ahead of time, so the call is spent on decisions instead of data entry. ACOG's telehealth tips for ob-gyn patients start here — "Ask what you will need to have with you during the visit" and "Write down your symptoms, health history, medications, and questions for your ob-gyn" ([ACOG, 10 Telehealth Tips for an Ob-Gyn Visit](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/infographics/10-telehealth-tips-for-an-ob-gyn-visit), accessed October 30, 2026).

Translated into log terms, the ahead-of-time bundle usually looks like this:

- **Your report, covering everything since your last visit.** The [2-page log book report](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report/) is built for this: a name header, a check-type grid, an over-target list with context notes, and a completion summary. Send it in whichever form your clinic prefers.
- **A short question list.** Two or three things you actually want interpreted — questions that live only in your head tend to stay there.
- **Context, not conclusions.** The American Diabetes Association suggests noting "what factors may have affected them, such as food, activity, and stress" ([ADA, Check Your Blood Glucose](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/treatment-care/checking-your-blood-sugar), accessed October 30, 2026). "Fasting, 6:40 am, slept badly" is data your doctor can use; what it *means* is their call.

Whatever channel your clinic uses — portal message, upload, plain email — send early enough that a failed upload leaves a day to retry. A refused file type is a clerical question, not a medical one.

## During the call: which page to put on screen

Portals are busy, and sent-ahead files do get missed; screen sharing is the fallback. Prepare it like this:

- **Open the report before the call, fullscreen.** ACOG covers the technology half: "Try out any technology you need for the visit ahead of time" ([ACOG, Telehealth Tips](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/infographics/10-telehealth-tips-for-an-ob-gyn-visit)). The document half is the same idea: know which file and which page before anyone joins.
- **Share the grid page first.** In the [two-page report](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report/), open the page with the check-type grid — rows for each check time your team ordered, columns for days. That's the scan view: a week of fasting and after-meal readings in position, absorbable in seconds.
- **Keep the over-target list one click away.** When your doctor asks about readings over the targets *you were given*, page to that list; the notes — "skipped dinner," "walked after lunch" — turn numbers into a conversation instead of a quiz. (Reading your own log calmly is its own skill — see [how to read your blood sugar log during pregnancy](/articles/blood-sugar-log-pregnancy/).)

If the visit is a phone call rather than video, ACOG's tips cover that too: "Let them know if you would prefer to do your telehealth visit by phone only." Numbers by voice still work when the report went through ahead of time.

## During the call: how to write down what your doctor says

Remote visits break an old habit: the doctor writes the adjustment into your chart while you're sitting right there. On a video call, the pen is yours. Capture, in your own words:

- **Any change to your check schedule** — which check times, how often, starting when.
- **Any change to your targets** — the numbers your care team sets for *you*. Write them exactly as stated and read them back: "So fasting moves to that new number starting tomorrow — did I get that right?"
- **Anything you were told to watch for** — in the doctor's words, not your interpretation of them.
- **Who said it, and on what date** — gestational diabetes care often involves more than one clinician.

Then two things in the minutes after: enter any new targets or check times into your log (or app) *before* your next reading — comparisons should follow your doctor's latest instructions, not last month's. And if something got said during a frozen frame, send a short portal message to confirm it. Nobody grades you for asking twice.

NIDDK's standing advice explains why: "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose. … Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([NIH NIDDK, Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed October 30, 2026). On a telehealth week, "take your tracker with you" means: open on your screen, or already in your doctor's inbox.

## Questions people ask about telehealth visits and their logs

**Do telehealth visits replace my in-person prenatal visits?**
That's your doctor's decision, not yours or ours. ACOG notes gestational diabetes brings more frequent prenatal visits; which can happen by video depends on your pregnancy and your clinic's policy.

**What if my clinic can't receive files through the portal?**
Email is an officially legitimate channel: ACOG's FAQ says logs "also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn." Ask which format they want. If every channel fails, the same two pages print cleanly for an in-person visit.

**How far back should the report I share cover?**
The default: everything since your last contact with the team. But ask once — preferences differ, and it's one of the questions the [report guide](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report/) suggests putting to your clinic directly.

**My telehealth visit is tomorrow and I haven't prepared anything. Now what?**
Send what you have — a CSV export or a clear photo of a paper log sheet is better than nothing, and ACOG's first tip is simply to ask the office what they need. One under-prepared visit doesn't undo your record.
