# Questions to Ask at Your Gestational Diabetes Appointments (+ Free Printable Agenda)

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Gestational diabetes comes with more appointments than a typical pregnancy — ACOG is direct about it: "You will need more frequent prenatal care visits to monitor your health and your fetus's health" ([ACOG, Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). Each one is short. The difference between leaving relieved and leaving with three unanswered worries is usually whether your questions were written down before you walked in.

The American Diabetes Association puts asking plainly: "Don't hesitate to ask questions or seek support — there are many effective ways to manage GDM" ([ADA, Gestational Diabetes](https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/gestational-diabetes)). This page is a question bank you can steal from, plus a printable agenda to carry the questions in. Note what's *not* here: answers. Every answer belongs to your care team — this list just makes sure the questions get asked.

## The agenda method (five minutes, night before)

1. **Capture during the week.** When a question pops into your head at 6am with a fasting reading in your hand, write it on the agenda, not on a sticky note that evaporates.
2. **Rank your top three.** Appointments are short. If everything gets asked, nothing gets answered thoroughly.
3. **Bring your log.** ACOG: "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)); NIDDK similarly: "Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([NIDDK, Managing & Treating](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)). Questions land better next to a readable record — [here's how to make one](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report/).
4. **Write the answers down, in the appointment.** Memory after a medical visit is famously optimistic. Future-you, at 6am tomorrow, needs the exact instruction.

## The question bank

### Targets and schedule

- **"What blood sugar targets are right for me?"** Worth asking because the authorities say so: NIDDK publishes reference ranges and still ends the paragraph with "Ask your doctor what targets are right for you" ([NIDDK, Managing & Treating](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)). Your targets are a medical instruction, not an internet lookup.
- **"Which check times apply to me, and what should I do if I miss one?"** The answer sets up your reminders. The *what-if* matters as much as the schedule — the missed-check panic is worse than the miss when you know the protocol in advance.

### Records and reports

- **"How would you like my numbers — paper, an app, or a printed report?"** Paper logs are officially fine: ACOG notes "blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). Your clinic has a preference; asking beats guessing. (If you want the trade-offs: [paper vs app, honestly](/articles/paper-log-vs-app-gestational-diabetes/).)
- **"Can I email the report the day before, so the visit starts at the discussion?"** Front desks have policies; this finds them out.

### Readings and context

- **"When a reading is over my target, what should I write down before I contact you?"** You're not asking what a number means — you're asking what *context* your team wants attached to it: food, sleep, timing, nothing. Their answer turns your log into something they can act on.
- **"Who on your team should I talk to about my eating plan?"** NIDDK: "Managing gestational diabetes includes following a healthy eating plan and being physically active" ([NIDDK, Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational)). *Who* builds that plan with you — dietitian, educator, OB — is exactly the right thing to ask, because the plan is theirs to build, not the internet's.

### The road ahead

- **"If my eating plan and activity aren't enough, what would we talk about next?"** NIDDK describes the sequence factually: "If your eating plan and physical activity aren't enough to keep your blood glucose in your target range, you may need insulin" ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational)). Asking what the path looks like is planning — and any decision about starting, stopping, or changing a medication belongs entirely to you and your care team, never to an app or an article.
- **"What follow-up testing will I need after the baby arrives?"** There is a standard answer to have ready: ACOG states, "If you have GD, you should have a blood test 4 to 12 weeks after you give birth. If your blood sugar is normal, you will need to be tested for diabetes every 1 to 3 years" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). Ask what applies to *you* and when they want it scheduled.
