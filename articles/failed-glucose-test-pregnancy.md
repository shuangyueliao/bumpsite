# Failed the Glucose Test in Pregnancy? What Happens Next — and What to Write Down

If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. You drank the orange drink, waited an hour, gave some blood — and got a call saying the result came back high. Take a breath: a failed screening step is the start of a **process**, not a verdict. This page walks through what that process usually looks like, and — because there is a stretch of waiting involved — what is actually worth writing down in the meantime.

## First: what the 1-hour screen is (and isn't)

The 1-hour glucose screen is a checkpoint, not a diagnosis. MedlinePlus (part of the U.S. National Library of Medicine) describes the routine version of the test: you don't need to prepare or change your diet beforehand, you drink a liquid containing glucose, and your blood is drawn 1 hour later ([MedlinePlus — Glucose screening tests during pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007562.htm)).

And here is the sentence we'd highlight on the page if we could: on the same page, MedlinePlus notes that **"Most women (about 2 out of 3) who take this test do not have gestational diabetes"** ([MedlinePlus — Glucose screening tests during pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007562.htm)). A high screen number sends you to the next step — it does not, by itself, mean you have gestational diabetes.

## The usual next step: a longer, more precise test

MedlinePlus describes the standard two-step sequence: "If your blood glucose from the first step is too high, you will need to come back for a 3-hour glucose tolerance test" ([MedlinePlus — Glucose screening tests during pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007562.htm)). The follow-up test is the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) — a longer appointment with a fasting start and several blood draws, which we walk through minute-by-minute in our [OGTT test-day guide](/articles/glucose-tolerance-test-pregnancy).

The exact cutoff that triggers the follow-up test varies by lab. As an example of the kind of number you might see, the MedlinePlus page lists 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L) as the level above which the next step is the OGTT — but **the cutoff printed on your own result sheet is the one that applies to you**. Don't compare numbers with a friend who was tested at a different lab; ask your provider what your lab uses.

Two practical things to confirm when they schedule the follow-up:

1. **Which version you're having.** Labs may use the 3-hour test with a 100-gram glucose drink or a shorter 2-hour test with 75 grams — MedlinePlus documents both ([MedlinePlus — Glucose screening tests during pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007562.htm)).
2. **The fasting instructions.** For the follow-up test, MedlinePlus notes you should not eat or drink anything except sips of water for 8 to 14 hours beforehand — follow your lab's own instructions on hours and water ([MedlinePlus — Glucose screening tests during pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007562.htm)).

## While you wait: three things worth writing down

The gap between the screening call and the follow-up test is often a week or more, and it's the most common time to spiral. Redirect that energy into a few records that are genuinely useful later:

- **What you eat and when.** Note each meal with the time you took the **first bite**, plus a rough description of the food. This is not for self-judging — it's for your dietitian if GDM ends up on the table. Food records only mean something with times attached, and "first bite" is the anchor care teams use for after-meal checks.
- **The appointment details.** Date, time, address, whether the test is the 2-hour or 3-hour version, and the fasting instruction in your lab's own words.
- **Your questions, in a running list.** Add each question as it occurs to you instead of trying to remember them on the day. The list at the bottom of this page is a starting set.

One thing **not** to do: start a drastic pre-emptive diet "to pass the test." Your care team wants the follow-up test to measure your normal physiology — if you change how you eat first, ask your provider first. (General eating guidance during pregnancy belongs to your care team; we don't prescribe food plans on this site.)

## If the follow-up test comes back elevated

If more than one of the results on the follow-up test is above the reference values, the lab and your provider will walk you through what that means — the diagnostic conversation belongs to them, not to an app or an article. When that day comes, our [first 7 days after a diagnosis](/articles/just-diagnosed-gestational-diabetes) picks up the story: capturing your instructions, setting up a log, and getting the most out of the next appointment. And to understand what your numbers on the result sheet mean in context — including why "context" never means self-diagnosis — see our guide to [blood sugar targets and who they belong to](/articles/gestational-diabetes-blood-sugar-targets).

## Questions to bring to the phone or the next visit

1. "What was my screening number, and what cutoff does my lab use for the follow-up test?"
2. "Which follow-up test am I having — the 2-hour or the 3-hour version — and what are the exact fasting instructions?"
3. "Should I keep eating normally until the test, or do you want me to change anything?"
