# Your Partner Has Gestational Diabetes: How to Help the Logging System Without Becoming the Food Police

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to her and her care team — always follow her doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If anything concerns either of you, contact her healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about her care, consult her OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of her care team.

The call came mid-pregnancy: she has gestational diabetes. You went looking for what to do, and nearly everything you found is written to her. This one is for you — the partner who wants to help without hovering. Some perspective that makes the job easier: gestational diabetes is one of the most common conditions of pregnancy — the CDC estimates that 5% to 9% of U.S. pregnancies are affected every year ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)) — and her care team will direct the medical side. Your side is the system around her: notes, timing, rides, records. Three jobs fit in that job description, and none of them requires a nutrition opinion.

## First, the one boundary that matters most

You are not the food police. Not by tone, not by raised eyebrow, not by "helpfully" reading labels out loud at the table.

Her eating plan is not yours to design or enforce. The CDC puts it plainly for women: "follow a healthy eating plan developed with your doctor or dietitian" ([CDC — Diabetes During Pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-diabetes/index.html)) — it's built with her clinicians, for her body and her pregnancy. When dinner turns into an inspection, the predictable result is guarded meals and a log with holes in it, which helps nobody — least of all the dietitian who is supposed to read it.

So here is the division of labor, stated once and held: **her care team manages the condition; you help the system around it.** Everything below stays on that side of the line.

## Job 1: be the second ear at appointments

Prenatal visits after a gestational diabetes diagnosis are dense — check schedules, instructions, referrals, follow-up dates. One person can't listen, advocate, and write at the same time. You can be the second set of ears.

- **Before the visit:** help her turn half-formed questions into a written list. Our [questions-to-ask guide](/articles/gestational-diabetes-questions-to-ask-doctor) is built for this — read it together the night before.
- **During the visit:** write things down verbatim. How many checks per day and at which times, who to call between visits, the next appointment date. If the team gives her target numbers, copy them exactly, units included — don't round, don't convert, don't interpret.
- **After the visit:** compare notes over lunch and add anything either of you missed to her log while it's fresh.

Some women prefer to handle visits solo. Ask her once what she'd like, then follow her lead — being a second ear is an offer, not a standing order.

## Job 2: be the reminder backup — quietly

The CDC's checklist for managing gestational diabetes includes "Go to all your prenatal appointments and follow your treatment plan" ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)). Appointments and check times are pure logistics. Your turf.

- **Set up the backup plan together.** "Do you want me to know your check times? How do you want reminding?" A shared calendar, a nudge at agreed times, or just keeping the appointment dates straight — her call, made in advance.
- **Nudge the way she asked.** "Want company for the evening check?" is backup. An announcement that it's test time, in front of other people, is surveillance. Same information; opposite effect.
- **Be the spare tire, not the steering wheel.** If her phone dies or she falls asleep, you're the reason a check or a visit happens on time. But the checks belong to her hands and her schedule.

One more date for the shared calendar: ACOG's FAQ says "If you have GD, you should have a blood test 4 to 12 weeks after you give birth" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). The reminder for that follow-up might literally be your job.

## Job 3: help with the meal notes — as scribe, not supervisor

Her food log has exactly one intended reader: the dietitian. It works best when it's fast, rough, and complete. You can help with the *recording* — none of the *judging*:

- **Be the scribe.** While she eats or rests, jot down what was on the plate, in plain words. No omissions, no rounding down, no commentary.
- **Note the first-bite time.** If her care team times after-meal checks from when she starts eating, that time is the anchor every after-meal number hangs on. "First bite 6:42" is one of the most quietly useful sentences you'll write all week.
- **Let the record be hers.** If she'd rather not log something, that's her call. A log she trusts you with is worth more than a log she feels watched over.

Want to go one step further? Eat what she eats. NIDDK notes that "Managing gestational diabetes includes following a healthy eating plan and being physically active" ([NIDDK — Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational)) — sharing the same dinner is solidarity, not strategy. And if her care team has okayed movement, an after-dinner walk you take together says support without saying a word. (Our [food log guide](/articles/gestational-diabetes-food-log) covers what the log should contain — written for her, useful for her scribe.)

## FAQ

**My partner was just diagnosed with gestational diabetes. What should I do this week?**
Read the first week together: our [just-diagnosed guide](/articles/just-diagnosed-gestational-diabetes) walks through days 0–7 as a set of things to write down. Then capture exactly what her care team said, keep the appointment list straight, and offer the food-note scribing if she wants it. The plan itself comes from her team — not from the internet, and not from you.

**Should I tell her what not to eat?**
No. Her eating plan is "developed with your doctor or dietitian" ([CDC — Diabetes During Pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-diabetes/index.html)). If she wants to talk food, the most useful sentence you own is: "Want to add that to the question list for your dietitian?" Then write it down. You're the scribe; the dietitian is the reader.

**What are her blood sugar numbers supposed to be?**
We don't publish target values — they're medical instructions that belong to her and her care team, and borrowing one from the internet or from someone else's pregnancy is a bad idea for the same reason. Write down exactly what her team says, in the units they use. Our [targets article](/articles/gestational-diabetes-blood-sugar-targets) explains why they're individual.

**What if she seems overwhelmed?**
Be an ear before being a fixer. Two facts help: ACOG's FAQ puts it this way — "For many women, a healthy diet and regular exercise will control blood sugar" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)) — and the CDC says "Managing diabetes during your pregnancy can help you have a healthy pregnancy and a healthy baby" ([CDC — Diabetes During Pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-diabetes/index.html)). If she's struggling with the routine, the numbers, or the worry, encourage her to raise it with her care team, and offer to come along.
