# When to Check Blood Sugar After Eating During Pregnancy: 1-Hour vs 2-Hour — and When the Clock Starts

After a gestational diabetes diagnosis, one of the first practical questions is deceptively simple: *when*, exactly, am I supposed to check after a meal? Your care team writes that schedule — this article doesn't change it. It explains the two after-meal formats you'll most often hear about, the timing detail most people forget to ask about, and how to record every check so your team's schedule actually shows up in your log.

## Why after-meal checks are part of the plan at all

Blood sugar checking is one of the standard parts of gestational diabetes management. The CDC's overview of gestational diabetes lists "checking your blood sugar to make sure your levels stay in a healthy range" among the ways the condition is managed during pregnancy ([cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html), accessed October 29, 2026). The NIH's National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) puts the tool in your hands: "Your health care team may ask you to use a blood glucose meter to check your blood glucose levels" ([niddk.nih.gov — Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed October 29, 2026).

The key phrase is *may ask*: how many checks per day, and at which moments, is prescribed person by person. Nothing here tells you when to test — it explains the formats so your team's instructions are easy to follow and record.

## 1-hour and 2-hour: the two after-meal formats

When you read official guidance, one thing stands out quickly: "after eating" is not one category — it's two, and different care teams use different ones.

The NIDDK's gestational diabetes management page lists example target categories this way: "Before meals, at bedtime, and overnight: 95 or less," "1 hour after eating: 140 or less," and "2 hours after eating: 120 or less" — and then immediately adds: "Ask your doctor what targets are right for you" ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed October 29, 2026). The American Diabetes Association's consumer page on treating gestational diabetes makes the same point with its own numbers: "the American Diabetes Association suggests the following targets for most women with GDM" — 95 mg/dL or less before a meal, 140 mg/dL or less one hour after a meal, 120 mg/dL or less two hours after a meal ([diabetes.org — How to Treat Gestational Diabetes](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/pregnancy/gestational-diabetes/how-to-treat-gestational-diabetes), accessed October 29, 2026).

Two things matter about those numbers here:

- They are **published examples, not prescriptions**. Both sources say it directly: the target that applies to you is the one your own care team gives you. Your prescription may match these examples or differ from them.
- They show that **"1 hour after eating" and "2 hours after eating" are distinct check types** that coexist in official guidance. Some plans use one, some the other, some both at different points. Your log needs to distinguish them — they're different rows in your care team's table.

This article takes no position on which format is "better" — that's a clinical question your team has already answered for you, or will.

## The detail people forget: when does the clock start?

Here's the practical wrinkle that "1 hour after eating" hides: an hour after *what*? From your first bite? From when you finish the plate? The exact moment the clock starts is a convention your care team uses, and it's worth confirming once and writing down.

Ask it at your first dietitian or doctor visit, verbatim: *"When you say one hour after a meal, do you mean from my first bite, or from when I finish eating?"* Then record the answer in your log setup.

Why this matters: meal logs — paper or app — record the time you **started** eating. If your team counts from the first bite, a start-of-meal timestamp makes every check time computable at a glance. If your team counts differently, note *their* convention instead — their instruction wins.

## What to write down with each after-meal check

Whatever the schedule, every check is worth four lines in your log:

- **When you started the meal** — the first-bite time, plus which meal it was (breakfast, lunch, dinner, snack).
- **The check time and type** — which slot of your prescribed plan this was (fasting, 1-hour, 2-hour, bedtime).
- **The number itself**, in the units your team uses (mg/dL or mmol/L — don't mix).
- **One line of context.** Activity is the classic example: ACOG's patient page on gestational diabetes notes "it's a good idea to add a walk for 10 to 15 minutes after each meal. This can lead to better blood sugar control" ([acog.org — Gestational Diabetes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed October 29, 2026). Recording whether you did gives your team context they can actually use.

One more habit: if a check runs late or gets missed, record it anyway with its *actual* time and mark it. An incomplete-but-honest log beats a sparse-but-perfect one. (Fixing entries later is covered in [reading your blood sugar log](/articles/blood-sugar-log-pregnancy/).)
