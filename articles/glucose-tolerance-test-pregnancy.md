# The OGTT in Pregnancy: the Test-Day Walkthrough, mg/dL vs mmol/L, and Keeping Your Numbers

If you've been sent for an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) during pregnancy, you've probably realized it's the longest single appointment of the whole pregnancy — blocked out hours, no eating beforehand, several blood draws. It's also the test that generates the most number-rich result sheet you'll receive this trimester. Here's the day in a realistic timeline, how to read the numbers on it, and why saving that sheet matters more than most people realize.

## Before test day

The OGTT starts from a fast. MedlinePlus describes the preparation for the 3-hour test: "Do not eat or drink anything (other than sips of water) for 8 to 14 hours before your test" ([MedlinePlus — Glucose screening tests during pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007562.htm)).

Your lab will give you its own instructions — window length, water rules, and whether to take usual medications (a question for them, not for this site). Two logistics notes that pay off:

- **Book the earliest slot you can.** An 8–14 hour fast ending mid-morning is easier than one ending mid-afternoon — and you're at the lab for at least 3 hours once it starts.
- **Bring something to do and a snack for immediately after.** MedlinePlus notes to "allow at least 3 hours for this test" ([MedlinePlus — Glucose screening tests during pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007562.htm)).

## The timeline on the day

The classic 3-hour version, as MedlinePlus describes it, runs like this: you'll drink a liquid containing **100 grams of glucose**, you'll have blood drawn **before** you drink it, and then **3 more times, every 60 minutes** after you drink it ([MedlinePlus — Glucose screening tests during pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007562.htm)).

So the shape of the morning is: fasting draw → glucose drink → draws at 1, 2, and 3 hours. Some labs use a shorter **2-hour, 75-gram** version instead — the same MedlinePlus page documents both the one-step (2-hour, 75 g) and two-step (3-hour, 100 g) approaches and their reference values. Which one you're having is written in your lab order; if it isn't obvious, ask when you book.

While you're waiting between draws, you generally stay put in the waiting area — following the staff's instructions, not anyone else's story about their own test day.

## Reading the result sheet: mg/dL vs mmol/L

Your result sheet will show one value per draw. In the United States it will usually be in **mg/dL**; many other countries use **mmol/L**. MedlinePlus defines them plainly: "mg/dL means milligrams per deciliter and mmol/L means millimoles per liter" ([MedlinePlus — Glucose screening tests during pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007562.htm)).

The two units describe the same reading at different scales, and converting between them is a one-step move: **divide mg/dL by about 18** to get mmol/L, or **multiply mmol/L by about 18** to get mg/dL. You can sanity-check the constant against any pair on your own sheet — for example, the MedlinePlus page lists the same thresholds in both units: 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L) for the 1-hour screen, and, on the 3-hour test, 95 mg/dL (5.3 mmol/L) fasting, 180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L) at 1 hour, and so on. "About 18" is a reading aid, not a precision tool — when a number actually matters, the unit your care team quotes is the one that counts.

If your meter and your lab turn out to use different units, you're not doing anything wrong — it's common enough that we made it a first-class setting: more on that below.

## How the results get read — by your provider, not by you

On the MedlinePlus page, the interpretation rule for the 3-hour test is that if more than one of your blood glucose results is higher than the reference value, the result points to gestational diabetes ([MedlinePlus — Glucose screening tests during pregnancy](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007562.htm)). What that means **for you** — including what happens next — is the conversation to have with your provider when they call. ## Keeping your numbers: the part everyone skips

An OGTT result sheet is a snapshot at five moments. The weeks after it are a continuous record — and they're far more useful to your care team if both live in the same place:

- **Photograph or scan the result sheet** the day you get it, and file it somewhere you can find it at 3 a.m.
- **Enter the values into your log** with their check types (that fasting draw, the 1-hour value, and so on). They become the baseline your daily checks sit next to.
- **Keep the units consistent** going forward, or log in both — mixing units across entries makes a log unreadable later.
- **Backfill anything you missed.** A log that allows late entry beats a log that punishes it; missed entries happen.
