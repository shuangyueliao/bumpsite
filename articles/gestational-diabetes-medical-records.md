# Your GDM Paper Trail: How to Get Copies of Your Own Pregnancy Records, and Keep Them With Your Log

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

A gestational diabetes diagnosis quietly turns a pregnancy into a documented one: a result sheet from the glucose tolerance test, a stream of lab reports, visit notes, ultrasound reports — and, alongside all of it, a glucose log that exists nowhere except your phone or your clipboard. Most people never see most of these documents; you can, and asking for copies is simpler than it sounds. Here's where each record lives, how to get your copies, and how to file the clinic's paperwork next to your own log.

## The paper trail a GDM pregnancy generates

A pregnancy managed for gestational diabetes produces more documents than a typical one — none of it exotic, just scattered:

- **Glucose tolerance test results.** The screening test — and the longer follow-up test, if yours requires one — ends in a lab report, held by the ordering practice and the lab. Our [test-day walkthrough](/articles/glucose-tolerance-test-pregnancy) covers the visit and the result sheet.
- **Daily check readings.** Your meter keeps its own memory; what your clinic holds depends on what you handed over at visits. Unless someone scanned it in, the between-visits record exists only because you wrote it down.
- **Visit notes, lab panels, and ultrasound reports.** The practice's chart — ultrasounds sometimes live at a separate office.
- **The delivery admission.** The hospital keeps its own chart for the birth stay.
- **Pharmacy fill history,** if prescriptions were part of your plan.
- **After birth, the birth certificate** — not a medical record but a legal one, issued by your state's vital records office (more below).

## Where to get copies, channel by channel

Each kind of record has a front door:

- **The patient portal first.** Lab results and visit summaries often appear there automatically. If you have portal access and haven't been downloading, start — save each report as a PDF the day you see it. Portals come and go with insurance and practice changes; the files you saved stay.
- **The practice's records office,** often called medical records or health information management. You'll fill in a short form — whose records, what date range, complete chart or summary, paper or PDF.
- **The hospital's records department** for the birth admission — hospitals are separate organizations from your OB's practice.
- **The lab,** which can usually reissue a missing report; the practice can tell you which lab it was.
- **The pharmacy,** for a printed fill history.

Two habits make every channel smoother: ask about fees and turnaround when you submit (they vary by office, state, and format), and request copies **before** you change practices, move, or switch insurance — records are easiest to get from the source you're still standing in.

## The two-track archive: clinical records plus your own log

Here's the model worth keeping all pregnancy: two archives, and neither replaces the other.

**Track one — the clinical record:** what was ordered, measured, and decided. The clinic owns the filing; you can own copies.

**Track two — your log:** the readings, meals, and notes from between visits. Most of it was never in the clinic's hands unless you brought it — the part of the story that exists only because you kept it.

MedlinePlus, the National Library of Medicine's patient information service, makes the same point in its [personal health records](https://medlineplus.gov/personalhealthrecords.html) guidance: "These charts are your medical records. They may be on paper or electronic," and, "To keep track of all this information, it's a good idea to keep your own personal health record."

Gluing the tracks together takes one folder and a few minutes a month:

- **Save or photograph every result sheet** the day it reaches you.
- **Enter key results into your log** with their check types and dates.
- **Annotate as you file** — the unit (mg/dL or mmol/L), the date, who produced the report. Future-you reads annotations.
- **Export your log regularly** and store it with the clinical copies — two places, not one.

The rule underneath: a log you can export is yours. A log you can't export is someone else's database with your name on it.

## Birth, and the years after

The reason to keep both tracks tidy is that the paper trail doesn't end at delivery — it changes shape:

- **Follow-up visits reference this history.** ACOG's patient FAQ on [gestational diabetes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) states: "you should have a blood test 4 to 12 weeks after you give birth," and, "If your blood sugar is normal, you will need to be tested for diabetes every 1 to 3 years." What schedule applies to you is your care team's decision — but those conversations are where an archive pays off: the clinician is asking about a period of your life for which you hold the day-to-day record.
- **The long-term picture is why the record is worth keeping.** The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html) notes: "About half of women with gestational diabetes go on to develop type 2 diabetes," and suggests talking to your doctor about how to lower your risk. Not a verdict on anyone's future — a reason that conversation happens, and a reason a dated record is useful to bring to it.
- **The birth certificate comes from your state.** CDC's National Center for Health Statistics keeps a [Where to Write for Vital Records](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/w2w/index.htm) directory, noting "The federal government does not distribute certificates, files, or indexes with identifying information for vital records." Your state's vital records office issues it — order extra copies while you're at it.

## FAQ

**Do I have to give a reason to get copies of my own records?**
Asking for your own records is a routine administrative request — offices handle it with a standard form, and you don't need a special reason to fill one out. If a request seems stuck, ask what's missing; most delays are form details, not pushback.

**Will copies cost anything?**
It depends. Fees, if any, and turnaround vary by office, state, and format — the two things to ask when you submit. Downloading results through a patient portal, where one is available, is typically the no-cost route for documents that appear there.

**What's the difference between my medical record and my glucose log?**
Your medical record is the clinic's version: what was ordered, measured, and decided at appointments. Your log is what happened between appointments — extra checks, meal notes, rough days. Your care team sees it only if you show it. Together they answer more questions than either alone.

**Can my own log become part of my medical record?**
Often, yes — many practices can scan patient-provided documents into your chart, and some portals let you add home readings yourself. It's a per-practice question: bring the log to a visit and ask, with dates and units labeled first.
