# How to Log a Blood Sugar Reading: the Complete Entry, Meter Memory, and Notes That Make Numbers Usable

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

The first home checks after a gestational diabetes diagnosis bring the lancet, the strip, the wait — and then a number on the screen, with no instructions about what to do next. Home checking is standard in this kind of care: ACOG tells patients, "You will use a glucose meter to test your blood sugar levels," and follows it with the less-discussed instruction — "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). This article is about building that record, one entry at a time: what a complete entry contains, how to transcribe from meter memory, and the notes that make numbers usable.

## What one complete entry contains

An entry is more than a number. NIDDK puts it plainly: "Make sure to keep a record of your blood glucose self-checks, including the date and time you checked it, the results" ([NIDDK — Managing Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/managing-diabetes)). In practice, a complete entry has five fields:

1. **The reading, exactly as the meter showed it** — no rounding, no converting. Note the unit displayed (mg/dL on most US meters, mmol/L elsewhere).
2. **The date and time the check actually happened.**
3. **The check type** — fasting, one hour or two hours after a meal, bedtime — whichever your care team assigned. Without it, a reading is hard to use later, because the types answer different questions.
4. **The meal it belongs to**, for a meal check, including your first-bite time. Your team decides how a meal check is timed; the first-bite time is the anchor the entry hangs on (our [after-meal timing guide](/articles/when-to-check-blood-sugar-after-eating-pregnancy) explains why).
5. **A short context note** when anything was different (more below).

NIDDK's GDM page adds the habit that holds the record together: "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose" ([NIDDK — GDM](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)). Checking without logging is how weeks go by with numbers your team never sees.

## The timestamp: record the time it actually happened

A reading is tied to its moment — the same number reads differently at 6:30 a.m. than at 9:00 after a breakfast that ran long. So log the real time, even when it looks off-schedule: keep a late check's actual time and note it ("check ~40 min late"); if you log later, use the meter's own timestamp, not the moment you're typing; and if the meter's clock was wrong — a fresh battery, a daylight-saving weekend — its stored times may be shifted too.

If the schedule seems unclear, that's a question for your team: CDC's advice is to "Ask how often to have your blood sugar checked to make sure you're on track" ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)).

## Transcribing from your meter's memory

Every home meter keeps a run of past results in memory, each stamped with date and time — your safety net for the forgotten entry, the one to double-check, or the week you fell behind. To transcribe accurately:

- Put the meter next to your log and work backward one reading at a time.
- Copy **every field as displayed**: number, unit, date, time. No converting, no "cleaning up."
- Read the finished entry back against the screen — the classic slip is a transposed digit (134 becoming 314), and ten seconds of checking catches most.
- If your meter pairs with an app or a computer, use that transfer; meal notes still get added by hand — meters remember numbers, not dinner.

An entry transcribed later is just as legitimate as one typed immediately: the log doesn't grade your speed, it keeps your record whole.

## Context notes that make numbers usable

The note field is where a log becomes context. Helpful notes are short, factual, and free of judgment:

- **First-bite time** for each meal a check is logged against.
- **What was different**: a long walk after dinner, a late lunch, a rough night's sleep, a fever, a stressful morning.
- **Medication, if your team prescribes any**: record name and time taken — as a record only; whether anything should change belongs to your care team.

A week with notes reads very differently for your ob-gyn than a bare column of values: "Your blood sugar log will help your ob-gyn provide the best care during your pregnancy" ([ACOG FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). Notes aren't confessions — two or three words is plenty.

## Paper, app, or both: pick what you'll keep up

There's no single right format. ACOG notes that "Blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn"; NIDDK points to "a chart or a smartphone app" as a way to keep track ([ACOG FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes); [NIDDK — Managing Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/managing-diabetes)). The best format is the one you'll still be using in week six — and whatever holds your log, "Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([NIDDK — GDM](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)).

## The 60-second routine

1. Check as scheduled.
2. Enter the number and unit exactly as displayed.
3. Attach the check type and the meal.
4. Confirm the time is the real one.
5. Add a one-line note if anything was different.
6. Done.

When a week of complete entries has piled up, the next skill is reading them calmly — patterns, not single numbers, plus fixing mistaken entries: see [how to read your blood sugar log during pregnancy](/articles/blood-sugar-log-pregnancy).

## FAQ

**I checked my blood sugar but forgot to write it down. Is the reading lost?**
Usually not. Most meters keep recent results in memory with date and time — open memory mode, find the reading, transcribe all fields. An entry entered an hour late beats a missing one.

**My meter shows mmol/L but US articles use mg/dL. Should I convert?**
No — record each number in the unit the meter displayed and keep track of which unit that is. Hand conversion invites transcription errors; our [mg/dL vs mmol/L guide](/articles/mg-dl-vs-mmol-l) explains how the systems relate.

**Should I log a check my care team didn't schedule?**
If you took an extra check, record it like any other — full fields, plus a note on why. What counts toward your plan, and how it's interpreted, is your care team's call.
