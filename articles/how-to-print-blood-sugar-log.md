# How to Print Your Blood Sugar Log: Getting GDM Numbers Onto Paper Your Clinic Accepts

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

You've been testing on the schedule your care team gave you, and the readings have been piling up in a meter, an app, or a notebook. Before your next prenatal visit, those numbers need one last step: getting onto paper or a PDF your clinic can work with. It takes about fifteen minutes. This article walks through the three routes — export, screenshots, and clean transcription by hand.

## Why your clinic still wants paper (or a PDF)

The record itself is standard care. ACOG's patient FAQ tells patients to "keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit," adding that "your blood sugar log will help your ob-gyn provide the best care during your pregnancy" ([ACOG, Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed October 30, 2026). CDC says the same in visit terms: "Go to all your prenatal appointments and follow your treatment plan" ([CDC, Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html), accessed October 30, 2026).

Digital records are officially fine too — the same ACOG page notes "blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed October 30, 2026). But visits are short, and scrolling a phone in an exam room eats minutes. A printed grid lets your OB take in a week of readings in seconds. NIDDK puts it simply: "Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([NIDDK, Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed October 30, 2026).

## Route 1: Export from your app, then print

Look for an export, share, or report option in your app's settings. Two things to hunt for:

- **A CSV export.** CSV is a plain spreadsheet file that opens in Numbers, Excel, or Google Sheets. Check that each row has a date, a time, a check type (fasting, after meals — whatever your team ordered), the reading, and the unit. Set the print layout to landscape, then print it or save it as a PDF and bring both.
- **A built-in report or print feature.** Preview before printing: a name header, a visible date range, and every reading next to its time and check type.

One practical note: not every logging app lets your data leave. Some collect readings for months and offer no export button, no print option, no way out with your own numbers. If you're still choosing a tool, make exporting the first-week test — enter a few readings, then try to get them back out.

## Route 2: Screenshots, when there's no export

If your app (or a meter's companion app) has no export, screenshots are the honest fallback:

- Capture the log view with **dates visible**, in date order.
- Keep **each reading next to its time and check type** in the frame.
- One or two days per screen reads better than thirty entries squeezed into one tall image.

Know the trade-off: images make your care team do the work — zooming, scrolling, transcribing. A handful is fine; twenty is slower than paper. If you can spare twenty quiet minutes, transcribe into a grid (next section) and let the screenshots be the backup.

## Route 3: Transcribing by hand, cleanly

If you're copying numbers onto a blank log — the [free printable blood sugar log](/articles/printable-blood-sugar-log/) is one place to start — a few habits make the result clinic-readable:

- **One consistent grid.** Dates down the side, one column per check time your team ordered.
- **Every reading gets a date, a time, and a check type.** "134, Tuesday" isn't recoverable; "Tue 7:10am, fasting" is.
- **One unit everywhere**, written once on the page (mg/dL or mmol/L — don't mix).
- **Copy readings as-is.** No rounding, no "fixing," no averaging inside the grid; a summary goes on a separate line below the raw numbers.
- **Leave gaps honest.** A missed check stays blank or gets a "not tested" note — never back-filled from memory.
- **Add one-line context where something was unusual.** CDC's monitoring page suggests exactly this: "You may want to keep notes about anything that might have affected your reading" ([CDC, Monitoring Your Blood Sugar](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/diabetes-testing/monitoring-blood-sugar.html), accessed October 30, 2026).

The habit underneath is NIDDK's first instruction: "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose" ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed October 30, 2026). Timely, labeled entries are what make the printout take fifteen minutes instead of an hour.

If you'd rather end up with the polished two-page version — name header, completion summary — that's a different question from getting the raw numbers out. Our guide to [turning a GDM log book into a report your doctor will actually read](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report/) covers what belongs in that document.

## What to bring to the appointment

- **The printed log or PDF**, with your name and the date range written at the top — loose unnamed printouts have a way of ending up in the wrong chart.
- **A backup copy** — the PDF or CSV on your phone, or emailed ahead if your clinic prefers that.
- **Your meter.** NIDDK notes your health care team "can show you how to use your meter" ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed October 30, 2026).
- **Your questions** — the printout is fuel for the conversation, not a substitute for it.

## FAQ

**Can I just show my phone instead of printing?**

Often, yes — ask what your clinic prefers. ACOG notes logs can be kept in apps and emailed to your ob-gyn ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed October 30, 2026). A printout is still the safer bet in the exam room: nothing depends on signal, battery, or a locked screen.

**How far back should the printout go?**

There's no official rule. Everything since your last visit is a sensible default; some teams want the full record since diagnosis. Ask — it's a one-line message, and it makes the visit smoother.

**My app has no export button. What now?**

Screenshots first (dates and check types visible), then a clean transcription into a grid when you have twenty quiet minutes. Going forward, put free CSV export on the checklist for any app you use — a log you can take with you beats a prettier one you're stuck inside.

**Can I print straight from my iPhone?**

Usually, yes. For a PDF or image, the share sheet's Print option works with most AirPrint printers. To make a PDF instead, share → Print, then pinch outward on the preview to save it for emailing or printing later.
