# How to Read Your Blood Sugar Log During Pregnancy: Patterns, Not Panic — and How to Fix a Mistaken Entry

The numbers add up over the next few months. The log they fill is not homework to be graded — it's the record your care team reads. ACOG tells patients directly: "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit," and "Your blood sugar log will help your ob-gyn provide the best care during your pregnancy" ([acog.org — Gestational Diabetes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed October 29, 2026). The NIH's NIDDK adds the habit that makes the record complete: "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose," and "Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([niddk.nih.gov](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed October 29, 2026).

This article is about the quiet skill nobody teaches: reading your own log without panicking, and fixing the mistakes that would otherwise distort it.

## Read patterns, not single numbers

The single most useful reframe: **one reading is a data point; a week is a pattern.** Any single number can be affected by a hundred ordinary things — what was on the plate, how much you slept, whether the check ran late. That's exactly why your team prescribed a *schedule* instead of a one-off test: repetition is what turns readings into information.

Three habits make a log readable:

- **Look at each check type separately.** Fasting and after-meal readings answer different questions — that's why official example targets treat them as different categories. NIDDK's page lists "Before meals, at bedtime, and overnight: 95 or less," "1 hour after eating: 140 or less," and "2 hours after eating: 120 or less" as published examples — immediately followed by "Ask your doctor what targets are right for you" ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed October 29, 2026). A week that mixes all three types into one column hides the very patterns your team is looking for.
- **Compare against the targets you set from your prescription — nobody else's.** When an app flags a reading as "over your target," the subject is *your* target, entered from your care team's instructions. A flag is not a verdict about your health — it's arithmetic against the number your doctor gave you. Interpretation belongs to your clinician.
- **Look at the week, then the month.** Most weeks in a GDM log are ordinary. Scrolling back is for catching the *repeat* — the same slot, the same shape, several days running. That's what's worth circling for your next appointment.

## What "time in range" means — and what it doesn't

If you use a logging app, you'll eventually meet the phrase **time in range**. Stripped of jargon: the share of your *planned* checks that fell inside the targets you set. That's all it is.

Two honest caveats belong right next to it:

- It's a **logging view, not a medical conclusion** — it summarizes what you recorded and doesn't replace your care team's interpretation.
- It's only as complete as your **check completion**. If half the scheduled checks never made it into the log, a high percentage means little — which is why "record everything" (see our [after-meal timing guide](/articles/when-to-check-blood-sugar-after-eating-pregnancy/)) beats any single number.

Treat the out-of-target list the same way: not a wall of shame, but a ready-made agenda — the specific readings your team will want to discuss.

## Fixing mistakes: why edit freedom matters

Real life comes with imperfect entries:

- You fat-fingered **134** as **314**.
- The 2-hour check happened 45 minutes late.
- You logged a brunch under "breakfast" and now the meal link is wrong.
- The bedtime check got forgotten — it belongs to yesterday.

Each is a normal human moment worth fixing — your log feeds decisions your care team makes. What matters is whether your tool lets you.

This is not a hypothetical. A public App Store review of the Gestational Diabetes Tracker app, posted January 15, 2025 (1 star), reads in full: "Terrible. Keeps telling me not in range of meal / Mea at 9:04 tested at 11:04 idk how it says it's not in range / Doesn't let you bypass and add it to your log" (quoted verbatim from the app's public US App Store reviews; review retrieved from the App Store reviews feed, on file October 28, 2026). The complaint isn't logging itself — it's the *refusal*: a tool that blocks a legitimate correction instead of recording it.

Before committing to a tool, check that it can do all of these without a fight:

- Edit **any field** of an existing entry — time, value, note — at any later point.
- **Re-classify** a check's type (a "fasting" entered as something else can become fasting).
- **Re-link** a reading to a different meal, or no meal.
- **Backfill** a missed check on any past day, with no time windows and no dead ends.
- Let you **change your target ranges** when your care team updates them — without rewriting what your past readings actually said.
