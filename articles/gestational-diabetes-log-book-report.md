# How to Turn Your GDM Log Book Into a 2-Page Report Your Doctor Will Actually Read

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

A gestational diabetes log book has one job at the end of the week: hand your care team a picture they can absorb in the few minutes they have. The records themselves are official homework — the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists tells patients to "keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit," adding that "your blood sugar log will help your ob-gyn provide the best care during your pregnancy" ([ACOG, Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). The format you hand that record over in is your choice — and it matters more than most people realize.

## Why "readable" beats "complete"

Gestational diabetes changes your appointment schedule: "You will need more frequent prenatal care visits to monitor your health and your fetus's health," per ACOG ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). More visits, but each one is still short. Your OB is not going to scroll through four weeks of individual entries on your phone — they scan for patterns: fasting numbers across a week, after-breakfast readings day by day, how often each check happened at all.

NIDDK puts the same idea in log terms: "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose. Your blood glucose records can help you and your health care team decide whether your diabetes care plan is working. Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([NIDDK, Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)). The record is for the *pair* of you — which means its value depends on how quickly your care team can read it.

## What a doctor-readable report contains

Clinics have used the same grid format for decades because it works. A two-page report that gets read in full has five parts:

- **A header that says whose it is.** Your name, your due date or gestational age, and the date range covered. Sounds obvious; loose printouts without a name get shuffled into the wrong chart.
- **One consistent grid.** Rows for each check time your team ordered (fasting, after meals, bedtime — whatever yours is), columns for days. One glance shows a week of readings in position.
- **A completion line.** "26 of 28 planned checks" tells your OB instantly whether the log reflects a real week or a gap they should ask about.
- **Context on the readings that went over the targets your team set.** Not an interpretation — a note. "Fasting 6:40am, slept badly" is data. What it *means* is your doctor's call, not the report's.
- **One unit, everywhere.** Every number in mg/dL or every number in mmol/L, labeled once. Mixed units in one grid are how misreadings happen.

## What to leave out

- **Screenshots.** Ten meter photos force your doctor to do the transcribing. Put the numbers in the grid instead.
- **Food photos.** A short food note next to a reading carries the information; seventeen photos of omelets do not.
- **Anything not tied to a time and a check type.** A floating "134" from last Tuesday is not recoverable information — nobody knows which check it belonged to. If a reading is missing its time, say so in the note rather than guessing.
- **Trend commentary.** You don't need to explain what the week "means." Your job is accurate, timely, labeled data; interpretation is the visit's agenda, and [these are the questions worth asking there](/articles/gestational-diabetes-questions-to-ask-doctor/).

## Questions to ask about your report

- "Do you prefer a printed grid, a spreadsheet, or an emailed PDF before the visit?"
- "How far back should the report cover — one week, two weeks, everything since we last met?"
- "Which check time do you look at first? I'll make sure that one's easiest to scan."
- "Is there anything you want me to record that I'm not recording now?"
