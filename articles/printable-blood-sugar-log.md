# Printable Blood Sugar Log (Free PDF) vs a GDM App: How to Pick in 5 Minutes

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

You've just been handed a daily checking schedule and told to write everything down. First decision: paper or app? This is a *format* choice, not a medical one — and official guidance treats both as legitimate. This article compares them honestly — including the cases where paper simply wins — and leaves the decision with you.

## Both formats are officially on the table

You don't have to take our word for that:

- The NIH's NIDDK publishes its own printable record sheet for exactly this purpose: "You can keep track of your blood glucose levels using My Daily Blood Glucose Record (PDF, 45 KB)" ([niddk.nih.gov — Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed October 29, 2026).
- ACOG's patient page on gestational diabetes acknowledges the digital side of the same habit: "Blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn" ([acog.org — Gestational Diabetes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed October 29, 2026).

A federal health institute ships a printable PDF; the ob-gyn professional association says logs can live in apps. Whatever you pick, the format isn't the point — the complete record is. (What your team actually does with that record is covered in our guide to [reading your blood sugar log](/articles/blood-sugar-log-pregnancy/).)

## Where paper wins

Paper has real advantages, and pretending otherwise would be selling:

- **Zero cost, zero setup.** Print a sheet, take a pen, done — no install, no account, no permissions to grant at midnight.
- **No moving parts.** No battery, no notifications to configure, no update dialog in week 30. It works identically on day 1 and day 90.
- **Privacy by default.** A sheet in a folder discloses nothing to any server, because it isn't connected to one.
- **Universally readable.** Any nurse, any clinic, any appointment — including the urgent one where your phone is at 4%.

The honest trade-offs: the arithmetic and the pattern-reading are all on you, rows run out mid-week, and your handwriting at 7 a.m. before coffee is your problem.

## Where an app wins

A good logging app earns its place with the boring stuff:

- **Reminders that match your schedule.** If your plan includes checks 1 or 2 hours after meals, an app can schedule those notifications from the meal itself — paper can't tap you on the shoulder. (Standard local notifications can be delayed or suppressed by Focus modes and permissions — no honest app promises perfection here.)
- **Structure for free.** Check types stay separated, meals link to readings, and a week of entries becomes a readable summary without you doing arithmetic.
- **Legible output.** A printed report with your name in the header and the clinic's familiar grid is faster for a busy provider to read than nine days of handwriting.
- **Corrections.** A mis-typed number can be edited; re-classified; re-linked to the right meal. Paper handles that with a pen and crossed-out lines.

## When the wrong app is worse than paper

Here's the part most comparison articles skip: a badly chosen app can actively push people *back* to paper. Don't take our word for that either — these are public US App Store reviews of the Gestational Diabetes Tracker app, quoted verbatim with their dates and star ratings (reviews retrieved from the App Store's public customer-review feed; copies on file October 28, 2026):

> "The ads are obnoxious" — 2 stars, March 15, 2026: "I don't mind banner ads at the bottom or top. But the pop up ones when I'm trying to use the app are enough for me to just write down my readings on paper."

> "Adds" — 1 star, August 22, 2025: "This app was doing fine but it started having adds"

> "The Ads Are Awful" — 2 stars, October 20, 2026: "I used this app for weeks with no Ads and then all the sudden it has ads. They're obnoxious. I can't stand them. This app was better without the 100 million ads"

Read those carefully: nobody stopped *logging* — they stopped using a tool that fought them and kept the data flowing on paper. Switching tools mid-pregnancy costs continuity, which is why the choice deserves five minutes *now*.

Before committing to any app, run a short filter: do recent reviews mention ads appearing after weeks of use? Is an account mandatory? Can you export your data? Is the price visible up front? Our [gestational diabetes app checklist](/articles/best-gestational-diabetes-app/) walks all nine points.

## The 5-minute decision

- **Mostly want something to carry to appointments?** Take the printable log. It's free, instant, and every clinic can read it.
- **Want reminders, patterns, and a printable report in one place?** Pick an app that passes the checklist — then test the reminder and export functions in week one, not week nine.
- **Can't decide?** Start with the PDF today and add an app later. If you start with an app, choose one with free CSV export — a log you can leave is a log you can trust.
