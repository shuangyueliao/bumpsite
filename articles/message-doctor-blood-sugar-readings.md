# Messaging Your Doctor About Blood Sugar Readings: the Portal Message That Actually Gets a Reply

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Between visits, the patient portal is where most communication with your OB's office happens. Plenty of company: "Every year, 5% to 9% of U.S. pregnancies are affected by gestational diabetes" ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html), accessed 2026-10-30). Your log is meant to travel, too — "Blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn" ([ACOG, Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed 2026-10-30). If you've sent your numbers and heard nothing back, the format — not you — is usually the reason. Here's a structure your care team can actually answer, and what to reasonably expect after you press send.

## Why some messages sit in the queue

Portal inboxes are triaged: messages get routed to whoever can answer them and batched around a full clinic day. A message that takes ten minutes to decode waits longer than one answerable in a sentence — triage working as designed, not your team ignoring you. Three things slow a message down: it's long, it asks several questions at once, or it can't be responsibly answered in writing ("what do these numbers mean?"). The ADA's encouragement holds: "Don't hesitate to ask questions or seek support — there are many effective ways to manage GDM" ([ADA, Gestational Diabetes](https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/gestational-diabetes), accessed 2026-10-30).

## The structure: a time range, a pattern, one question

A message your team can act on has three parts and fits in a few sentences.

**1. Open with the time range.** "Over the past seven days…" or "Since my appointment on the 12th…" A bounded window tells the reader which slice of your log to picture — and stops the message from growing to cover the whole log.

**2. Describe the pattern you see — as an observation.** You see your log most often, so your description helps — as long as it stays a description. Compare against the targets *your* team gave you, note how often, leave interpretation out: "Most of my fasting numbers this week have been over the target you set for me, even on nights when dinner ran early." That's data. "Is something wrong?" is interpretation — not something a written reply can cover fairly. (Reading your own log calmly: [patterns, not panic](/articles/blood-sugar-log-pregnancy/).)

**3. End with one specific, answerable question.** One message, one question. The fastest-answered questions have short answers — a yes or no, a preference, a next step. "Would you like me to keep logging these nights, or would a quick call be easier?" beats a general "What should I do?" And "please schedule a visit" is a legitimate reply, not a brush-off.

**A template to copy (fill the brackets, delete the rest):**

> Hi — over the **[time range]**, I've noticed **[which check time, how often, compared to the targets you gave me]**. My full log is **[attached / exported / up to date in the app]**. My question: **[one question]**. Thank you!

The template avoids actual numbers and judgment — both belong to your care team.

## What to send with it — and what to leave out

Your message lands better when the record behind it is one click away. NIDDK's instruction is written for visits, but the idea travels: "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose," and "Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([NIH NIDDK, Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed 2026-10-30). For a message, the portable version is a clean export your team can check against your summary.

Leave out:

- **Every individual reading.** The thread carries the pattern; the log holds the readings. If your clinic wants raw numbers pasted in, they'll say so — format preference is a good appointment question ([a whole bank of those here](/articles/gestational-diabetes-questions-to-ask-doctor/)).
- **The two-page report.** A before-visit report is a different document for a different channel — [here's what goes into that one](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report/). The portal message gets the one-sentence version.
- **Multiple questions.** Two in one message is how both go unanswered — send the second separately.
- **A long story.** Context helps in a phrase ("slept badly, dinner ran late"), hurts in paragraphs.

## What to reasonably expect after you send it

Async means later, not lost. Offices answer portal messages in batches, often at set times, and reply times vary by practice — if your clinic publishes an expected turnaround, that's the number to go by. Three things you *can* reasonably expect:

- **A morning send usually moves sooner.** Messages that arrive before the batch get seen with it. That's queue logistics, not medicine — and some practices route messages through a nurse line first.
- **The reply may be a phone call, not a message.** Plenty of teams answer reading questions by phone — an unfamiliar number on your caller ID may be the answer you asked for.
- **Silence past the expected window deserves one follow-up.** Reply inside your original thread ("Following up on my message from Tuesday"), then call the front desk if the thread stays quiet. Threads get lost in office systems — a fact about inboxes, not a verdict on you.

And the rule above all: the portal is the slow lane. For anything that can't wait, the phone line your clinic gave you for urgent concerns is the right channel; emergencies belong to emergency services. Which is which is your clinic's call.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a portal message about my blood sugar readings be?**
Three to five sentences: the time range, the pattern, one question, and a pointer to your log. An honest draft running six paragraphs wants an appointment, not a thread.

**Should I message every time a reading is outside my targets?**
Worth asking directly — some teams want per-reading alerts, most prefer a pattern summary with the log attached. The comparison is against *your* team's prescribed targets. Official pages publish example ranges and still end with "Ask your doctor what targets are right for you" ([NIH NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed 2026-10-30).

**What if my message gets no reply at all?**
One follow-up in the same thread after your clinic's expected window, then a call to the front desk. If the question can't wait, skip the portal and use the urgent line your clinic gave you.

**Can I send a spreadsheet or photos of my meter instead of typing numbers?**
You can offer it: "Would a CSV export or photos work better for you?" ACOG notes logs "also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed 2026-10-30) — a small question worth settling once.
