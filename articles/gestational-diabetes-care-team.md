# Your Gestational Diabetes Care Team: Who Does What, Who Sees Your Log, and Who to Call

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

The appointment where you learn you have gestational diabetes usually ends with a stack of names: the OB, a referral to a registered dietitian, maybe an endocrinologist, a diabetes educator, and a number to call "if anything comes up." Here is that org chart — what each person does, how your log travels, and who to call. Lineups vary between practices, so treat this as a map of usual roles: your team's own answer, asked directly, is the one that counts.

## Why gestational diabetes is managed by a team

Gestational diabetes adds work to a pregnancy that was already full time: monitoring, an eating plan, a check schedule, more appointments. ACOG is direct about the schedule: "You will need more frequent prenatal care visits to monitor your health and your fetus's health" ([ACOG, Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). The CDC's treatment list is equally plain: checking blood sugar, healthy eating, staying active ([CDC, About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)).

No single person covers all of that — hence the referrals. And the specialists would say so themselves: NIDDK writes that "You are the most important member of your health care team" ([NIDDK, Managing Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/managing-diabetes)). You're the one checking, recording, and showing up — the roles below are scaffolding.

## The roles, one by one

**Your obstetrician (ob-gyn) — the center of the plan.** Your OB owns your pregnancy care overall, and the log lands here most officially. ACOG tells patients to "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit," because "Your blood sugar log will help your ob-gyn provide the best care during your pregnancy" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)).

**Registered dietitian — the eating plan.** This is not a diet you find online: the CDC describes an eating plan "developed with your doctor or dietitian" ([CDC, Diabetes During Pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-diabetes/index.html)), and in many clinics the dietitian is the main reader of your food notes — keep those notes rough and honest rather than curated.

**Diabetes care and education specialist — the skills coach.** Often called a diabetes educator, this role covers the how: meter technique, what to record, what a surprising reading warrants. Per the CDC, "You'll work with a diabetes care and education specialist who will help you create a plan and teach you about the 7 key self-care behaviors" ([CDC, About DSMES](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/education-support-programs/index.html)). If someone reviews readings between OB visits, it's often this person — confirm, don't assume.

**Endocrinologist and other specialists — when your team adds them.** Some people are referred to an endocrinologist or a maternal-fetal medicine specialist; many are not. If a referral lands in your lap, ask: what does this specialist add, and do medication decisions stay shared with my OB? Any decision to start, stop, or change medication belongs to your doctors — "Talk with your health care professional about what treatment is right for you" ([NIDDK, Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)).

**The supporting cast.** NIDDK's general description of a diabetes team includes "a pharmacist to help you with your medicines" and "a diabetes educator, nurse, or registered dietitian who can tell you more about your disease and how to improve your self-care" ([NIDDK, Managing Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/managing-diabetes)).

## Who sees your log — and how it travels

The record's official path runs through every visit: ACOG says to bring your log to each prenatal visit and notes that "Blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)); NIDDK says the same from the other side: "Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([NIDDK, Managing & Treating](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)).

What varies is the between-visits arrangement — readings sent weekly, reviewed at appointments only, or routed through a patient portal. All are workable; the mistake is guessing. Ask right after diagnosis: *who receives my numbers, how often, and in what format?* One log, one source of truth, and a named reader — that's the whole system. ## Who to call for what

| Your question or situation | Usually the first call |
|---|---|
| Scheduling, referrals, insurance paperwork | The front desk |
| Meter technique, what to record, log format | Diabetes educator or dietitian |
| The eating plan — foods, meals, timing | Dietitian (using the plan your team set) |
| Your target numbers, or what a reading means | The doctor who set them — NIDDK: "Ask your doctor what targets are right for you" |
| Starting, stopping, or changing any medication | The doctor who prescribed it — before anything changes |
| A reading or symptom that concerns you | Your provider's line — ask in writing what needs a same-day call |
| A medical emergency, for you or the baby | Emergency services (911 in the US) |

Not sure who to call? The front desk routes these all day.

## When two team members give you different answers

It happens: a meal tip that doesn't match an OB instruction, a portal message that reads differently from the educator's advice. You're not the referee: write down both, note the conflict, and ask the OB, who holds the overall plan, to reconcile them. A readable log makes that a two-minute conversation, because everyone is looking at the same numbers.

## Making the record easy for all of them

Everyone above reads the same record; the highest-leverage work is keeping it clean: every reading with time and check type, short food notes, a context line when something was unusual. ## FAQ

**Do I have to see an endocrinologist if I have gestational diabetes?**
Not automatically. Many people are managed throughout by their OB with a dietitian or diabetes educator; some teams add a specialist. It depends on your history and your practice's plan — ask your OB what the plan is for you.

**Who actually reads my blood sugar log?**
Whoever your team designates. ACOG instructs patients to bring the record to each prenatal visit; in many clinics the educator or dietitian reviews readings between visits — arrangements differ, so ask. "Who receives my numbers, and how?" is an ordinary front-desk question.

**What's the difference between a dietitian and a diabetes educator?**
Broadly: the dietitian builds and adjusts your eating plan — the CDC describes it as developed "with your doctor or dietitian" — while the educator teaches self-care skills like monitoring. Job splits vary by clinic, so ask yours who handles which.

**What if I have questions but no appointment coming up?**
Ask anyway — the ADA puts it simply: "Don't hesitate to ask questions or seek support" ([ADA, Gestational Diabetes](https://diabetes.org/about-diabetes/gestational-diabetes)). Write questions down as they come up, keep the log current, and use the contact path your team gave you.
