# Blood Sugar Levels in Pregnancy: Every Number You'll See, What It Measures, and Who It Belongs To

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Somewhere between your first prenatal visit and your third trimester, blood sugar numbers start showing up — on a lab printout, on a meter screen, in an appointment summary. If you searched for what those numbers are *supposed* to be, here is our honest answer: this page doesn't list target values, and that's deliberate. There is no single "normal" number that fits every pregnancy, and the values that apply to you come from your own care team — not from an article or an app. What we can do is walk you through every kind of number you're likely to meet: what each one is for, where it belongs, and whose job it is to interpret it.

## The numbers you'll meet, at a glance

During a typical pregnancy, four kinds of blood sugar numbers appear, each with a different job and a different owner:

| The number | What it measures | Where it gets recorded | Who explains it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Screening result | How your body handled one timed glucose test | Your prenatal chart (lab report) | Your OB provider |
| Diagnostic (OGTT) results | Whether the diagnostic criteria are met | Your prenatal chart (lab report) | Your OB provider |
| Home readings (fasting, after meals) | Your day-to-day pattern between appointments | Your blood sugar log | You and your care team, together |
| Postpartum test result | Where your blood sugar stands after delivery | Your chart after birth | Your postpartum provider |

Now, one at a time.

## The screening number: one timed test, one snapshot

Most pregnant people meet their first blood sugar number at a screening test late in the second trimester. The CDC notes that gestational diabetes "usually develops around the 24th week of pregnancy" and that testing for it typically happens "between 24 and 28 weeks."

What does this number measure? A snapshot: how your body handled glucose at one timed point. That's all. A screening number is a checkpoint, not a diagnosis — it only decides whether the next, more precise step is worth doing. If yours comes back flagged, the most useful sentence is on the CDC's page: "You'll need to be tested to know for sure if you have gestational diabetes." We walk through that step in [what to do after a failed glucose test](/articles/failed-glucose-test-pregnancy).

## The diagnostic numbers: the OGTT

If the screen suggests a closer look, the follow-up is the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) — a longer appointment with several timed measurements. The way NIDDK (the NIH's diabetes institute) describes the sequence: "If the results of the glucose challenge test show high blood glucose, you will return for an OGTT test to confirm the diagnosis of gestational diabetes."

Two things worth knowing. First, these results are compared against formal diagnostic criteria — work that belongs to your provider, drawing on the lab's reference ranges. An app or a blog can't diagnose you, and neither can you; that's not a limitation, it's the design. Second, the OGTT produces several numbers, not one, and labs differ in how they run it. For the appointment itself, our [test-day guide to the glucose tolerance test](/articles/glucose-tolerance-test-pregnancy) covers it minute by minute.

## The numbers you collect at home: fasting and after-meal

If your care team asks you to monitor at home, a new set of numbers enters your life. These readings measure your day-to-day pattern: how your blood sugar behaves between appointments, with your real meals.

First: **each home reading has a check type attached to it**, and the type is part of the number's meaning. A fasting reading (after waking, before eating) and an after-meal reading (counted from your first bite) describe different things — a log that records "a number" without "which kind of number" loses half its value. More on timing in [when to check blood sugar after eating during pregnancy](/articles/when-to-check-blood-sugar-after-eating-pregnancy).

Second: **these numbers belong to your care team, together with you.** The CDC's management guidance includes "Checking your blood sugar to make sure your levels stay in a healthy range," and ACOG's FAQ describes tracking readings with a glucose meter and keeping a log to review with your ob-gyn. Which values count as "in range" for you, and how often to check, are your provider's call — not a chart on the internet's. That division of labor is the subject of our guide to [blood sugar targets and who sets yours](/articles/gestational-diabetes-blood-sugar-targets).

## The unit question: mg/dL or mmol/L?

One reason these numbers confuse people: the same measurement is written two ways depending on where you are. US labs and meters report in **mg/dL**; much of the rest of the world uses **mmol/L**. Neither is wrong — they're different rulers for the same thing. If a friend's numbers look ten times smaller than yours, that's almost certainly why. Before carrying any number between apps, logs, or countries, see [mg/dL vs mmol/L: how the two units relate](/articles/mg-dl-vs-mmol-l) — accidental unit mixing is one of the most common log mistakes.

## The numbers after birth

One more number lands after your baby arrives. ACOG's guidance is specific: after gestational diabetes, "you should have a blood test 4 to 12 weeks after you give birth," and depending on the result, testing "every 1 to 3 years" may follow. These numbers are about your long-term health and are just as worth recording — our guide to [keeping your GDM log after birth](/articles/after-birth-gdm-log-archive) explains how to archive and reuse it.

## FAQ

**So what *is* a normal blood sugar level during pregnancy?**
Official organizations publish example values for each test, and your provider can share the ones that apply to you. We don't reprint thresholds on purpose: the values that count for you depend on your history, your lab, and your team's judgment. Our [targets guide](/articles/gestational-diabetes-blood-sugar-targets) explains how published examples and your team's instructions differ.

**My screening result was flagged. Does that mean I have gestational diabetes?**
No — a screening result is a signal that the follow-up test is recommended, and as NIDDK describes, that's the test that confirms a diagnosis. About the two-step process itself, see [what happens after a failed glucose test](/articles/failed-glucose-test-pregnancy).

**Why do my numbers look so different from my friend's?**
Most often: different units. mg/dL and mmol/L describe the same measurement with different scales. See [mg/dL vs mmol/L](/articles/mg-dl-vs-mmol-l) before comparing — or before retyping anything into an app.

**Do I really need to write every number down?**
You need the ones your team asks you to collect — and they're only useful if they're complete: the value, the check type, the time, and ideally a note about the meal. Whether you use a notebook or an app matters less than capturing those four things consistently.
