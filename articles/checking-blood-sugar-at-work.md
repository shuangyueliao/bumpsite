# Checking Blood Sugar at Work: Logging Around Shifts, Meetings, and Coworkers' Questions

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

A gestational diabetes diagnosis usually arrives mid-pregnancy — the middle of a working life. The plan your care team hands you includes blood sugar checks, and your workplace does not pause for them. This article is logistics, not medicine: fitting prescribed checks around shifts and meetings, logging honestly when the day runs long, and handling coworkers' questions with whatever privacy feels right. Whether to disclose your diagnosis at work, and what to ask for, are decisions for you and your care team.

## What your care team decides, and what is left to you

Your plan, not articles, settles the clinical side. The CDC lists checking blood sugar among the ways gestational diabetes is managed, and its monitoring page says plainly: "Your doctor will tell you when and how often to check your blood sugar levels" ([CDC — Monitoring Your Blood Sugar](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/diabetes-testing/monitoring-blood-sugar.html), accessed 2026-10-30). NIDDK describes the tool: "Your health care team may ask you to use a blood glucose meter to check your blood glucose levels" ([NIDDK — Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), accessed 2026-10-30). The ADA frames targets the same way: "You and your healthcare team will decide" what is right for you ([ADA — How to Treat Gestational Diabetes](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/pregnancy/gestational-diabetes/how-to-treat-gestational-diabetes), accessed 2026-10-30).

No official page covers where those checks happen between stand-ups and shift handovers — that part is yours to design.

## Fitting checks around shifts and meetings

- **Map your plan against your calendar once.** Event-anchored checks — waking, meals, bedtime — ride along with any schedule; fixed clock times are worth laying against a workday. A fingerstick takes a couple of minutes; knowing where those minutes live removes most of the friction.
- **Keep a small kit at your workstation.** Meter, lancets, strips, tissues — a pencil pouch beats a full case. Strips are sensitive to heat and moisture — the CDC advises "Keep the container closed tightly when you're not using your test strips" — so a desk drawer beats a summer glovebox, and shift workers do better with a kit in the bag.
- **Wash your hands first, especially at work.** The CDC's instruction: "Wash your hands with soap and warm water, and dry well before each test." Office hands meet fruit, snacks, and sanitizer all day — the break-room sink is worth the walk.
- **After-meal checks:** when the countdown starts after a desk lunch is covered in [our after-meal timing guide](/articles/when-to-check-blood-sugar-after-eating-pregnancy/).

## The honest log: what to do when the day runs late

NIDDK's record-keeping rule is simple: "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose." At work, the honest version has two clauses.

**When a check runs late**, log it anyway — actual time plus a note ("ran 45 min late, client call"). Visible, explained gaps beat a tidy-looking log with silent holes: your team can only read what you recorded.

**When a check is missed entirely**, leave it blank — never backfill an estimated number. Whether a late check still "counts" is a two-line question for your team, not a desk judgment call.

A sixty-second end-of-day ritual — fill in anything checked but never logged — pays off at appointments. ACOG: "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed 2026-10-30).

## Privacy and discretion: how visible you are is your call

Testing at work is quick, with more location options than it first appears: your desk if you're comfortable there, a private office or wellness room, your car, or a single-occupancy restroom with a sink. None is the "right" one — the right one is wherever the routine actually happens.

Visibility is also a software choice. The CDC notes you "can keep a written record" — but a paper log on a shared desk advertises itself, while a phone log looks like every other app on your screen. ACOG agrees logs "can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn." Some people on continuous glucose monitors simply glance at a reader — more in [our CGM in pregnancy guide](/articles/cgm-pregnancy/). Whichever you choose, run the [privacy checklist](/articles/gestational-diabetes-app-privacy/) first.

## When coworkers ask

Most people never notice, and most who notice don't ask. When someone does, you have comfortable options: matter-of-fact ("It's part of my prenatal care — routine stuff"), light deflection ("Just keeping an eye on some pregnancy things"), or as much detail as you'd like.

The boundary worth holding: your medical information is yours. Testing quietly is a normal choice, and so is talking about it openly. If you decide to tell a manager or close teammates, the conversation can make meetings and coverage easier to arrange — but it stays your decision, ideally with your care team's input. And if your schedule makes the prescribed checks genuinely hard, your log documents that reality for the team that can respond.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to tell my employer I have gestational diabetes?**
That is your decision, with your care team's input — this article doesn't push either way. Some people log at work without announcing anything; others find that telling one person eases the schedule. Both are fine.

**A meeting lands right when I'm supposed to check — what now?**
Check as close to the scheduled time as the meeting allows, and log the reading with its actual time plus a "ran late" note. Unsure whether late still counts? Ask your care team.

**Where can I test privately at work?**
A private office, wellness room, your car, or a single-occupancy restroom — wash your hands with soap and warm water first, wherever you are.

**Can I keep my log on my phone instead of paper at my desk?**
Yes — ACOG notes logs "can be kept online, stored in phone apps." A phone log stays on your screen, travels between desks and shifts, and exports a file your OB's office can read.
