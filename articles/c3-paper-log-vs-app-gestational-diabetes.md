# Paper log vs app for gestational diabetes: an honest comparison (including when paper wins)

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

You're going to spend three to six months writing down glucose readings, several times a day, on your care team's instruction. The [American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) tells patients to "keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit" — and adds, helpfully, that "blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn." Paper or app: both are officially legitimate.

Most comparisons you'll find online are written by someone selling one of the two options. This one isn't neutral by accident — we make a GDM app, and we're still going to tell you exactly when paper wins. You know your life better than we do.

## What the log is for (the part both options must do)

Whatever you use, the log has three jobs. The [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment) sums up the first: "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose." The second is context — the [ADA](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/treatment-care/checking-your-blood-sugar) suggests noting "what factors may have affected them, such as food, activity, and stress." The third is delivery: getting a readable record in front of your care team at the next visit. [ADA](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/treatment-care/checking-your-blood-sugar) again: "When you bring your log to your healthcare provider, you'll have a good picture of your body's response to your diabetes care plan."

Any option — paper notebook, printable PDF, spreadsheet, app — is judged on those three jobs, and nothing else.

## Where paper genuinely wins

- **Zero cost, forever.** A printed log sheet or a $2 notebook can't be beaten on price. We offer a free printable log PDF for exactly this reason — it's on our site.
- **Zero privacy surface.** A sheet of paper never syncs, never breaches, never gets shared with an advertiser. If this is the week you don't want one more app on your phone, paper is a completely sound answer.
- **No battery, no updates, no lockouts.** Paper works during a phone repair, a hospital stay, or a dead battery on check day.
- **Some people keep it up better.** The best log is the one you actually fill in. If tapping through an app annoys you and scribbling on a clipboard doesn't, the clipboard is the right tool.
- **It's a reasonable long-term home.** Some people use paper for their entire pregnancy and are happy with that. That's a legitimate finish line, not a failure state.

## Where an app earns its keep

- **Reminders.** Missed checks are the most common way a good log loses its value. An app can nudge you at your care team's check times and start a countdown after a meal. (Honesty clause: these are local notifications — Focus modes and permission settings can delay or suppress them, so don't rely on any app as your only reminder.)
- **The math does itself.** An app compares each reading against the targets you entered and shows per-check-type patterns and a simple time-in-range share — no manual charting, no arithmetic at the doctor's office.
- **Mistakes are fixable.** Transposed digits, a fasting reading logged as post-meal, a check you couldn't log until that evening — a good app lets you edit any field, reclassify the check type, and backfill. Paper's error handling is a strikethrough.
- **Delivery to your care team.** [ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) explicitly mentions logs that are "emailed to your ob-gyn." An app that exports a clean PDF grid or a CSV makes that a two-minute task instead of a transcribing session.
- **The record compounds.** Weeks of entries become trends you can see — which is the whole difference between a pile of numbers and a log you can read.

## A middle path that works for a lot of people

You don't actually have to declare a winner. A common pattern: log in whichever format is fastest at 6 a.m. (often the app, for the reminder alone), print or export the PDF grid before appointments, and keep a paper sheet on the fridge for the week's at-a-glance view. Paper and app aren't rivals; they're different ends of the same pipeline. If you start with paper and switch to an app (or the reverse) at week 20, nothing is lost — the record is yours either way.

## Questions to bring to your care team

1. "Do you prefer getting my log as a printed sheet or a digital file?"
2. "Would a PDF grid or a CSV spreadsheet be easier for your system?"
3. "Is the amount of detail in my current log right, or would you like more context noted?"
