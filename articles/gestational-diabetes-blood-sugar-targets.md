# GDM Blood Sugar Targets: Why the Numbers on Your Log Belong to Your Care Team

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Sooner or later, every gestational diabetes log confronts you with the same moment: a reading appears, the app or paper chart asks "compared to what?", and you realize a target number has to come from somewhere. The wrong answer is a forum. The right answer is your care team — and this page explains *why* that's not a cop-out, what published example values actually are, and how to use targets in a log without letting them run your head.

## What "target" actually means in a GDM log

A target range is a reference line your care team sets so that your recorded readings can be read *in context* — fasting against the fasting reference, after-meal readings against the after-meal reference. Two structural things follow from that:

1. **The comparison is informational, not a verdict.** A reading sitting above your target is a data point worth noting (and, if it keeps happening, worth asking about) — it is not a diagnosis, a grade, or an emergency in itself. What to do about readings lives entirely in your care team's hands.
2. **Targets are prescribed, like everything else in your plan.** The CDC lists checking blood sugar among the ways GDM is managed, and the same page makes clear the plan — food, activity, checks, and any medication — runs through your doctor ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)).

## Where published example values come from

Professional organizations do publish reference values — they're the shared vocabulary clinicians use when they set *your* plan. The American Diabetes Association's Standards of Care states, for gestational diabetes: "Glucose goals are fasting plasma glucose <95 mg/dL (<5.3 mmol/L) and either 1-h postprandial glucose <140 mg/dL (<7.8 mmol/L) or 2-h postprandial glucose <120 mg/dL (<6.7 mmol/L)" ([ADA — Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026, Section 15: Management of Diabetes in Pregnancy](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S321/163918/15-Management-of-Diabetes-in-Pregnancy-Standards)). The same document's goals table notes that the GDM values trace to the Fifth International Workshop-Conference on Gestational Diabetes Mellitus, and that goals should be individualized in consultation with care — the ADA text explicitly contemplates less stringent goals where the standard ones can't be met safely.

**Read those numbers as a specimen, not a prescription.** They show what such values look like in the professional literature; they are not this site's recommendation for you, and they are not automatically your targets. Your chart may match them, or differ meaningfully — because of your history, your medication plan if you have one, your units, or your team's own protocol.

## Three pieces of evidence that targets genuinely vary

If targets were universal constants, borrowing them from the internet would be harmless. They aren't:

- **The post-meal slot itself is a choice.** The ADA text above offers *either* a 1-hour *or* a 2-hour postprandial goal — different practices adopt different conventions, which alone produces two different-looking logs ([ADA Standards, Section 15](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/49/Supplement_1/S321/163918/15-Management-of-Diabetes-in-Pregnancy-Standards)).
- **Units differ.** The same reference value reads differently on a US meter (mg/dL) than on a lab sheet from abroad (mmol/L) — see our [OGTT and units guide](/articles/glucose-tolerance-test-pregnancy) for the conversion.
- **Individualization is part of the standard.** The ADA's own text makes individualization explicit, and the CDC's management pages consistently route the plan through your doctor rather than through fixed numbers ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)).

## How to use targets in a log (without the anxiety spiral)

Once your team has given you numbers, targets do their best work inside a consistent record:

- **Enter them once, per check type.** Fasting gets its own reference; each post-meal convention gets its own; bedtime (if assigned) gets its own. One number can't serve all slots.
- **Let the log do the arithmetic.** "24 over target" is easier to scan at a glance than re-deriving it from raw numbers every time.
- **Read patterns, not single points.** One fasting reading above target is a note; a stretch of them is a question to bring to your next appointment. The log exists to make that distinction visible.
- **Keep the history intact when targets change.** Care teams adjust targets over a pregnancy — when yours change, your log should keep your *original* recorded comparisons alongside the new line, so trends stay readable across the change.
- **Bring the log, not the worry.** The most productive sentence at an appointment is "here are my last two weeks, with context notes" — not a memorized feeling about one bad morning.
