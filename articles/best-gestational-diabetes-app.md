# The Best Gestational Diabetes App (2026): a 9-Point Checklist Instead of a Ranking

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Let's be honest about this headline. You searched for the "best" gestational diabetes app, and any page that answers with a single crowned winner is selling you something — including us, because we make one. So this article does the opposite of a ranking: it gives you a **9-point checklist** you can run against *any* app in about fifteen minutes, and at the end we hold our own app against the same list and let you keep score. No other product is named — a checklist doesn't need villains.

What actually matters is the deliverable, not the app. As ACOG puts it: "Your blood sugar log will help your ob-gyn provide the best care during your pregnancy," and "Blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn" ([acog.org — Gestational Diabetes](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes), accessed October 29, 2026). The app is a means; the complete, readable log is the end.

## The 9-point checklist

### 1. Ads — and where your attention goes

Some free apps are ad-supported, which means a medical logging flow shares the screen with things designed to be tapped. Check recent App Store reviews for the word "ads" and whether the tone gets worse over time — advertising that appears *after* weeks of use is a recurring theme in one-star reviews in this category.

### 2. Account requirement

Does the app work without signing up? A mandatory account means your health data sits behind credentials on someone else's server. A no-account app has no profile to breach, no password to forget at 2 a.m., and no marketing email in your inbox for the next decade.

### 3. Where your data lives

Read the App Store privacy label — it's on every product page, and it's the fastest honest summary you'll get. "Data Not Collected" means exactly that. If the label shows health data linked to your identity, phone number, or email, ask yourself who benefits from that linkage and where it travels.

### 4. Free tier and data lock-in

Two questions that prevent the classic trap: Can you keep logging after a trial ends? And does your *history* stay visible? A paywall that hides your own medical record mid-pregnancy is a wall built across your care team's line of sight. Logging should stay free; subscriptions should pay for outputs, not access to your past.

### 5. Data portability

Can you export everything — every reading, meal, and note — to CSV or a full backup file, for free, any time? Portability is your exit right. An app that holds your data hostage holds your leverage too.

### 6. Reminder behavior

If your checking plan includes after-meal checks, does the app schedule reminders from the meal itself? Then read how the app *talks* about reliability: standard local notifications can be delayed or suppressed by Focus modes and permission settings. Wording that promises reminders will "never" fail is marketing; wording that acknowledges system limits is engineering honesty.

### 7. Price transparency

Where does the price appear — on the store page, or buried in terms of service? And do the math in pregnancy terms: what does the app cost over the weeks you'll actually use it, with the $6.99-per-month style tier multiplied out versus a 6-month option? A price you can find without digging is the baseline.

### 8. Device support

Check the minimum iOS version listed on the App Store page. Some apps require the newest system version, which quietly excludes older but perfectly good phones — the iPhone SE (2nd generation), XR, and XS generation and everything after. If your phone is your only device, this can be the deciding row.

### 9. Units and report format

Does the app support both mg/dL and mmol/L, in case your lab results and your app disagree on units? And the end-of-loop question: what does the printed report actually look like? A grid your clinic recognizes — your name in the header, readings in date order, check types separated — gets read. A screenshot collage doesn't.

## How to run the checklist in 15 minutes

1. Open the app's App Store page: privacy label (point 3), minimum iOS version (point 8), and pricing section (point 7).
2. Read the most recent ~20 reviews, not the featured ones — recency is where the "ads showed up later" stories live (point 1).
3. Install, log one real meal and one reading, then try the three dishonesty detectors: edit the reading's time, export your data, and print the report (points 4, 5, 9).
