# The GDM food log: record meals so your dietitian can actually use them

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Somewhere in your first weeks after a gestational diabetes diagnosis, you'll likely sit down with a dietitian or nutritionist on your care team. That appointment goes very differently depending on what you bring. A vague memory of "mostly fine this week" gives them nothing to work with. A food log — meals, times, and the glucose readings next to them — gives them the raw material their job runs on.

One boundary note before anything else: **this article is about recording, not prescribing.** What to eat, how much, and when is the plan your care team builds with you. The [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment) puts it exactly that way: "Your health care team will help you make a healthy eating plan," and "the plan will help you know which foods to eat, how much to eat, and when to eat." Your job in this article is narrower and very doable: keep a record good enough that their plan can actually improve.

## Why the food log matters at all

Food choices, amounts, and timing are, per the [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment), "all important in keeping your blood glucose levels in your target range" — which is precisely why your care team wants to see the food side next to the numbers side. A glucose reading alone is half a sentence. The same reading next to "toast and eggs at 7:10, walked 15 minutes after" is a complete sentence they can respond to.

The [American Diabetes Association](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/treatment-care/checking-your-blood-sugar) makes the same point about every check: "write down your results and note what factors may have affected them, such as food, activity, and stress."

## What to write down (so the log is usable)

- **The time you started eating.** For post-meal checks, timing labels only mean something against the start of the meal — record the first-bite time so your log matches however your care team counts it.
- **The meal type.** Breakfast, lunch, dinner, snack. Patterns by meal are usually the first thing a dietitian looks for.
- **The foods, roughly.** "Rice, beans, chicken, avocado" is enough. You do not need grams unless your dietitian asks for them later. Rough-and-consistent beats precise-for-three-days-then-abandoned.
- **The readings, linked to the meal.** When you check after a meal, the reading should be attached to that meal in your log — that pairing is the entire point.
- **Context notes.** A short walk after eating ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) notes that adding "a walk for 10 to 15 minutes after each meal" is a common suggestion worth discussing with your team), poor sleep, stress, being sick — anything that would explain an unusual reading.
- **Medication notes, if any, as records only.** If your care team has you on medication, note the name and time as given to you. What to take and when is never a logging app's call.

## "Rough is fine" — the consistency rule

The most common failure mode of a food log isn't imprecision — it's quitting. Three perfect days of weighed portions followed by silence is less useful than six weeks of honest one-line meal notes.

A few things that keep it sustainable:

- Log **right after eating**, not at the end of the day. Thirty seconds now beats reconstruction later.
- Use **short forms** you'll remember ("oatml + milk + blueberries").
- Skip the judgment. A food log is data, not a report card. If a week runs chaotic, log the chaos — that's exactly the week your dietitian can use.
- Keep food and readings **in one place**, in time order. Two separate systems means the pairing never happens.

## What happens at the appointment

The [American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) tells patients to "keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit" — and your food notes ride along with them. When your dietitian can see which meals preceded which readings, the conversation shifts from "try to do better" to specifics: which meals they'd like to look at more closely, what detail they want added, what to change in the plan. [ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) also notes that eating regular meals — often "three meals and two to three snacks per day" as a general suggestion — is typical of the plans they describe, which is one more reason your log should include snacks, not just meals.

## Questions to bring to the dietitian

1. "Here's my food log with the readings attached — what patterns do you see?"
2. "How much detail do you want in the food notes — and do you want portion sizes, or is a rough description enough?"
3. "Should I log snacks and drinks too, or meals only?"
4. "What would you like me to change about how I record, so next visit is more useful?"

A food log isn't homework you're graded on. It's the shared notebook that makes your care team's advice specific — and specific advice is what actually moves a log.
