# Free Gestational Diabetes Apps: What 'Free' Costs Your Data, Your Attention, and Sometimes Your Export

It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

A gestational diabetes diagnosis comes with homework: check your blood sugar on a schedule and keep a record. "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit," as ACOG puts it ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). So you search for a free app — and find dozens.

Free is a sensible place to start — pregnancy expenses add up fast, and paying monthly to write down numbers is not a requirement of good care. But "free" is not costless: the bill arrives in one of three currencies — your attention, your data, or your export. And if you were diagnosed recently, some perspective: "Every year, 5% to 9% of U.S. pregnancies are affected by gestational diabetes," per the CDC ([cdc.gov](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)), and the ADA keeps it plain: "GDM is treatable, manageable," and "so know you're not alone" ([diabetes.org](https://diabetes.org/living-with-diabetes/pregnancy/gestational-diabetes)).

## What the app is actually for

The deliverable is the log, not the app. NIDDK, the NIH's diabetes institute, is direct about the mechanics: "Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)). ACOG blesses digital formats, too: "Blood sugar logs also can be kept online, stored in phone apps, and emailed to your ob-gyn."

So a free app has one job: capture readings and meals reliably, and hand the record over intact at every visit — everything else is overhead. One boundary: the app isn't the one that sets your numbers. NIDDK's instruction is "Ask your doctor what targets are right for you." An app that pre-loads targets or suggests doses is guessing about *your* pregnancy.

## Cost #1: your attention

The most visible price of free is advertising. Ad-supported apps are free because advertisers pay for access to you, and in a logging flow that trade lands at awkward moments. The pattern users describe in this category's store reviews is consistent — full-screen ads after saving a reading, videos before basic actions unlock, ads that appear only after weeks of use, when deleting the app feels expensive. Timing is the tell: ads on day one are a known trade; ads that move in at week 20 of a logging streak are timed to a captured user. Not every ad-supported app is obnoxious — free-with-ads is a fair deal for plenty of people. The probe: read the 20 most recent store reviews and search for "ads."

## Cost #2: your data

The quieter currency is data. Even ad-free apps commonly carry analytics and advertising toolkits — third-party code collecting device identifiers, usage patterns, and session timing. Combined with a health topic, that stream becomes unusually sensitive and can flow onward to companies you never signed anything with. The two-minute check: the App Store's "App Privacy" label lists what's collected and whether it's linked to your identity — "Data Not Collected" is the phrase apps that gather nothing can display — and the developer's privacy policy can be searched for "advertis," "analytics," "third part," and "share." We go deeper on all three data architectures in [Gestational Diabetes Apps and Your Data](/articles/gestational-diabetes-app-privacy/).

## Cost #3: your export

The sneakiest cost is the export wall. Log faithfully for weeks, then need your data *out* — switching phones, your clinic wants a printed record, or you've decided to move on — and some free apps reveal the wall: no export, export reserved for subscribers, "export" that means screenshots instead of data, or history that turns read-only after a trial. This matters more than it sounds: the log is part of your prenatal care, and NIDDK's instruction is to take your tracker to your care team. A log you can't leave with is a log you don't own. The question is short: *can I export everything to CSV, today, on the free tier, without paying?*

## The five-minute self-check

Before you trust any free app with a pregnancy's worth of readings, run this cost audit — five questions, five minutes, five practice entries:

1. **Attention:** log one meal and one reading — how many ads or upgrade prompts crossed your path?
2. **Data:** what does the privacy label say leaves your device, and is an account required first?
3. **Export:** can you get all your data into a CSV right now, free, without paying?
4. **History:** is your full past readable without a subscription — and what happens to it if you stop paying?
5. **Exit:** can you edit an entry's time and delete your practice entries cleanly?

This list asks the narrower question — *what does "free" bill you, and in what currency?* For the fuller feature-by-feature method (reminders, units, reports, pricing), see the [9-point checklist for choosing a gestational diabetes app](/articles/best-gestational-diabetes-app/).

## So is a free app ever just free?

Yes — honest free exists. A permanently free logging tier funded by a paid tier that adds *outputs* (like a print-ready clinic report) is a fair structure: the free side does the essential job, and you pay only for extras you choose.

That's the principle we built BumpSugar on: logging, viewing your full history, and CSV export sit on a permanent free tier — your data isn't locked behind a subscription or held hostage if you stop paying; the paid tier adds outputs, not access to your past. Whichever app you pick, the goal is unchanged: a complete, readable log you carry to every prenatal visit.

## FAQ

**Are free gestational diabetes apps safe to use with my health data?**
It depends on the app's architecture, not its price. Some free apps store everything on your device and collect nothing — the privacy label will say so. Ad-supported ones typically send usage and device signals to third-party toolkits even when your readings stay local.

**Can I just use a paper log instead of an app?**
Yes — ACOG's advice works just as well on paper, and paper shows no ads and collects no data. Apps add reminders, backup, and export; pick whichever you'll keep up with, because a consistent log is the goal.

**What about apps that come with built-in glucose targets or meal plans?**
Treat those as generic defaults, not guidance for you — NIDDK's instruction is to "Ask your doctor what targets are right for you." An app that logs, shows, and exports your readings is doing its job; one that pushes targets or doses is overstepping.
