# Referred to MFM for Gestational Diabetes: What the Referral Process Looks Like, and What to Bring From Your Log

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Somewhere between the exam room and your kitchen table, a referral slip to "MFM" changes texture. In the appointment it was one line on a form; at home it can feel like a verdict. This article walks through the referral *process* — the paperwork, the phone call, the first appointment, the records that travel with you — and leaves the medical meaning of your referral to the team that made it. A web page cannot tell you why *you* were referred; your care team can, so put that question first.

## What a referral is, in process terms

A referral is a routing slip: your OB has asked another set of obstetric eyes to join your care and send their findings back. The slip does not diagnose you; it directs you.

The CDC's summary of caring for diabetes during pregnancy fits in one sentence: "see your doctor as recommended and monitor your blood sugar levels" ([CDC, Diabetes During Pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-diabetes/index.html)). After a gestational diabetes diagnosis, its practical advice reads: "Go to all your prenatal appointments and follow your treatment plan" ([CDC, About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)). A referred appointment is still one of those appointments.

Medically, the *why* belongs to your team. Logistically, the *how* belongs to the rest of this page.

## The referral process, step by step

**1. Read the slip for logistics, not meaning.** It carries the practice's name, address, and phone number. If the reason line is a code, leave it alone — that line is written for schedulers, not for you.

**2. Call and schedule.** The front desk books these visits all day. Ask: Do my records need to be sent before the visit? What should I bring? How long will it last?

**3. Let your records travel ahead of you.** Most practices request records from your OB directly. What no office can pull from another office's chart is the record *you* keep — your day-to-day readings, food notes, and questions. That part travels in your bag.

**4. The first appointment.** Expect a longer conversation than a routine prenatal visit: your history, the records, and what happens next. The MFM team will explain what they recommend and why — that is the appointment's job, not this article's. On treatment questions, hold onto NIDDK's line: "Talk with your health care professional about what treatment is right for you" ([NIDDK, Managing & Treating Gestational Diabetes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)).

**5. The loop back.** Ask both offices how findings will reach your OB and who owns which decisions. You shouldn't spend your pregnancy as the messenger between two charts.

## What to bring from your log (the part you control)

At an MFM appointment, the only piece you personally assemble is your log. NIDDK: "Record the results every time you check your blood glucose" and "Take your tracker with you when you visit your health care team" ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/management-treatment)). ACOG, from the OB side: "Keep a record of your blood sugar levels and bring it with you to each prenatal visit," because "Your blood sugar log will help your ob-gyn provide the best care during your pregnancy" ([ACOG, Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)).

For a specialist visit, bring a small kit:

- **Your complete glucose log** — every reading with its time and check type. Missed one? Note why: a gap with a reason reads as data, not silence.
- **Short food and context notes** — one line per meal, plus a note when something was unusual ("slept badly," "long shift"). Rough and honest beats curated.
- **A one-page summary report** — a print-ready version of the log that a new-to-you practice can scan in minutes. [Here's how to build one, free](/articles/gestational-diabetes-log-book-report/).
- **Your medication and vitamin list** — names, prescribed doses, and who prescribed them. The list is for showing; questions about changing anything go to your doctors.
- **Your written questions** — [a ready-made list lives here](/articles/gestational-diabetes-questions-to-ask-doctor/).

## Questions to write down before the appointment

The referral's medical meaning is a question *for* your team, so put it on the list:

- "Can you help me understand why I was referred to MFM?"
- "What will happen at the first appointment, and roughly how long will it take?"
- "How will you and the MFM team share my records and my plan?"
- "While I wait, is there anything you'd like me to keep recording or bring?"
- If medications come up, ACOG notes that "Changes to your medication may be needed throughout your pregnancy to help keep your blood sugar in the normal range" ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)) — so "who decides what, and who do I call?" is a fair question to ask.

Opening more browser tabs will not tell you why *you* were referred; writing the question down and asking it directly will.

## FAQ

**Does an MFM referral mean something is wrong?**
A search page cannot answer that, and neither can this article. Only your care team knows why they referred you, and "Can you walk me through why?" is an ordinary question for them. On paper, the referral is a request for another specialist to join your care and report back — the process itself is routine.

**Will the MFM team replace my OB?**
Usually the MFM team works *alongside* your OB, with the overall plan still held by your OB. The split varies by practice, so ask both offices how they'll share records and decisions.

**What should I bring to the first MFM appointment?**
The referral paperwork, your ID and insurance card, your medication and vitamin list, your complete glucose log with food notes, a one-page summary report, and your written questions. The log and the report are the pieces only you can supply.

**What if the appointment is weeks away?**
Keep logging — a filled-in tracker is the most useful thing you can carry into that room. Confirm with your OB's office what to do in the meantime, and use the contact line they gave you if anything concerns you before the visit.
