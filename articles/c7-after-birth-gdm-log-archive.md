# After birth: your GDM log as an archive — follow-up tracking and taking your data with you

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is general information, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BumpSugar is a logging and reminder tool, not a medical device. It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Every person who logs their way through gestational diabetes eventually types some version of the same search: *does gestational diabetes go away after birth?* It's a reasonable question — and answering it for you is exactly what this article won't do. Whether *your* gestational diabetes has resolved, and what testing you need after delivery, are conclusions only your care team can make about you.

What this article can do honestly is the part nobody else covers: your log. Right now it's a working tool. After birth it becomes something different: a complete, dated record of a nine-month period that follow-up appointments, future pregnancies, and your own memory will all want back. Here's what official guidance says happens after birth (cited, so you can read it at the source), and how to make sure your record survives the transition.

## What official guidance describes after birth

These are attributed facts from public health authorities — the schedule that applies to *you* is your care team's call, and none of this is a recommendation from us:

- The [ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) patient FAQ states: "you should have a blood test 4 to 12 weeks after you give birth," and "If your blood sugar is normal, you will need to be tested for diabetes every 1 to 3 years."
- The same [ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) page describes the stakes in percentages: "One third of women who had GD will have diabetes or a milder form of elevated blood sugar soon after giving birth," and "Between 15 and 70 percent of women with GD will develop diabetes later in life."
- The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html) puts it plainly: "About half of women with gestational diabetes go on to develop type 2 diabetes," and suggests asking your doctor "how often to have your blood sugar checked."
- The [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational) notes the follow-on family picture: "Your child is more likely to have obesity or develop type 2 diabetes."
- [ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) also flags the next pregnancy: "GD greatly increases the risk of developing diabetes in your next pregnancy."

Read those together and the pattern is clear: postpartum life after gestational diabetes isn't an epilogue — official guidance treats it as an ongoing follow-up story. Which is precisely why the record you kept deserves to survive.

## Why your pregnancy log becomes an archive

- **Follow-up appointments reference your history.** The postpartum blood test and the retesting rhythm that [ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) describes are conversations between you and a clinician — and clinicians ask better questions when the pregnancy record is in the room.
- **The next pregnancy starts from this one.** With the elevated next-pregnancy risk that [ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) describes, an earlier conversation is likely — and last pregnancy's log (what your targets were, when they changed, what your weeks looked like) is the single most useful document you can bring to it.
- **Memory edits itself.** Six months after delivery, "my fasting numbers were always fine, it was meals that were hard" may or may not match what your log actually says. The log doesn't argue; it just shows.

## The archive checklist (about 20 minutes, do it once)

1. **Export everything as CSV.** CSV opens in any spreadsheet — Excel, Numbers, Google Sheets — and outlives any app. In BumpSugar this export is **free, permanently**; the same is true of the **JSON full backup**, which preserves your complete structured history. Data portability here is a right, not a paywall — your history is never locked behind a subscription.
2. **Save every PDF report you generated for appointments.** The printed clinic-style grid from each trimester is a snapshot a spreadsheet can't reproduce — it shows the summary your care team actually saw.
3. **Write down your target ranges per check type, with dates.** Targets can change over a pregnancy. A one-line note — "weeks 28–34: fasting 95, 1-hour 140, per Dr. __" — keeps the archive interpretable years later.
4. **Store it in two places.** Email the files to yourself, drop them in a cloud folder, print a copy for the folder with the lab results. Your log lived on one device during pregnancy; the archive shouldn't live on one device after it.
5. **Note the units and the app.** One line: "logged in mg/dL in BumpSugar, iOS, exported [date]." Future-you (or a new practice's intake nurse) will thank present-you.

1. "What follow-up testing schedule applies to me, and when should we plan it?" (The [ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes) page describes general guidance; your schedule is what your care team sets.)
