# What Is Gestational Diabetes? An Overview Focused on What You'll Be Tracking

It never recommends medications or insulin doses, and nothing here should be taken as advice to start, stop, or change any medication. Target ranges and check schedules belong to you and your care team — always follow your doctor. Reminders use standard local notifications and may be delayed or suppressed by Focus or notification permissions — do not rely on BumpSugar as your only reminder to test. If a reading or symptom concerns you, contact your healthcare provider. This article is educational only — for questions about your own care, consult your OB/GYN, endocrinologist, or another member of your care team.

Most explanations of gestational diabetes stop at the definition and leave you staring at a meter. This overview covers the standard ground — what it is, why it happens, how common it is — and then goes where you actually live: **what your care team will probably ask you to track.**

## The definition

Gestational diabetes (GD or GDM) is diabetes that is first recognized during pregnancy. ACOG's patient page is direct: "Some women develop diabetes for the first time during pregnancy. This condition is called gestational diabetes (GD)" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). The CDC frames it the same way: gestational diabetes "can develop during pregnancy in women who don't already have diabetes" ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)).

## Why it happens (and why it isn't your fault)

Pregnancy changes how the body handles insulin. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) explains: pregnancy hormones and changes like weight gain make the body's cells use insulin less well — insulin resistance — and "all pregnant women have some insulin resistance during late pregnancy." Most people can make enough extra insulin to overcome it; some cannot, and that gap is gestational diabetes ([NIDDK — Gestational Diabetes: Symptoms & Causes](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes/gestational/symptoms-causes)).

This is hormones and biology doing what they do in late pregnancy — not a report card.

## How common, and when it's found

The CDC reports that "every year, 5% to 9% of U.S. pregnancies are affected by gestational diabetes" ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)). If you've just been diagnosed, you're in large company — and the testing that found it is routine.

Timing follows the biology. Because it "usually develops around the 24th week of pregnancy," testing typically happens between weeks 24 and 28 — earlier if you're at higher risk ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)). If you came here after a screening test that came back high, our [what happens next guide](/articles/failed-glucose-test-pregnancy) covers the follow-up process.

## What managing it looks like (in your care team's hands)

The CDC describes GDM management in two parts — daily habits, and care your provider directs. On the daily track: "Eating healthy food in the right amounts at the right times," being physically active ("being active lowers your blood sugar"), and "checking your blood sugar to make sure your levels stay in a healthy range" ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)). On the provider track: "your doctor may prescribe insulin, metformin, or other medication" ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)) — whether medication enters the picture is entirely a conversation between you and your care team, and it's a good topic for the question list at the end of this page.

The CDC's own framing is worth holding onto: "Managing gestational diabetes can help make sure you have a healthy pregnancy and a healthy baby" ([CDC — About Gestational Diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/gestational-diabetes.html)). This is a manageable condition, not a catastrophe.

## What you'll actually be tracking

Here's where GDM gets concrete. ACOG tells patients: "You will need to track your blood sugar and do things to keep it under control," and "You will use a glucose meter to test your blood sugar levels" ([ACOG — Gestational Diabetes FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes)). In practice, a GDM log has four recurring ingredients:

1. **Blood sugar readings, each tagged with a check type.** The common slots in GDM logs are fasting (waking), 1 hour after a meal, 2 hours after a meal, and bedtime — which your team assigns, and how many per day, is their call.
2. **Meals, with first-bite times.** After-meal checks are timed from the start of eating, so the meal time is what makes those readings interpretable.
3. **Context notes.** A walk after dinner, a bad night's sleep, a longer-than-usual lunch. Short notes make a week of numbers make sense.
4. **The target numbers your care team gives you.** Published reference values exist, but the ones on your chart are the operative ones — see [why the numbers on your log belong to your care team](/articles/gestational-diabetes-blood-sugar-targets).

Notice what's *not* on this list: interpreting your own numbers, adjusting anything based on them, or diagnosing yourself. The log's job is to capture; the care team's job is to interpret. Keep those roles separate and the whole experience gets calmer.

## After your baby is born

The story usually ends well: ACOG states plainly that "GD goes away after childbirth," and MedlinePlus notes that high blood sugar levels "often go back to normal" after delivery ([ACOG FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes); [MedlinePlus — Gestational diabetes](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000896.htm)). Follow-up is real, though: the CDC advises getting tested for diabetes 4 to 12 weeks after the baby is born, and then having blood sugar checked every 1 to 3 years even if levels return to normal ([CDC — Diabetes During Pregnancy](https://www.cdc.gov/maternal-infant-health/pregnancy-diabetes/index.html)). Your log from these months becomes the archive those visits build on.

1. "What target numbers should I use, for which check types, and in which units?"
2. "How many checks per day — and which slots: fasting, after meals, bedtime?"
3. "What level of physical activity is right for me?" (ACOG's patient page describes general activity recommendations; the right level for you is an individual question — [ACOG FAQ](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/gestational-diabetes).)
